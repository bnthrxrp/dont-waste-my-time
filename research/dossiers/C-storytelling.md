# Research dossier C (Layer 1, unvalidated)

**Research question.** What structures and sentence-level practices make executive documents and presentations faster to understand and act on, and how strong is the evidence? Covers Pyramid/SCQA/BLUF, executive summaries, Rogers and Lasky-Fink brevity research, plain language (ISO 24495-1, plainlanguage.gov), assertion-evidence slides, Mayer's multimedia principles, Tufte and CAIB on slides, Amazon narrative memos (documented vs folklore), action titles, storyline and appendix use.

## Key findings

### C1. Writing for Busy Readers: Communicate More Effectively in the Real World (excerpt, 'When Writing for Busy Readers, Less Is More')

- **Source:** Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink, unknown, <https://behavioralscientist.org/when-writing-for-busy-readers-less-is-more/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** In a book excerpt, Rogers and Lasky-Fink report their own field experiment with 7,002 U.S. school board members: an invitation to a short survey written in 49 words drew a 4.8% response rate, versus 2.7% for a 127-word version. The excerpt reports no significance tests. It also reports that 29% of concise-email recipients expected the survey to take under five minutes, against 15% of wordy-email recipients (sample size not stated for that item).
- **Interpretation (agent):** The mechanism the authors propose (length signals effort; readers quit long messages early) is speculative per the excerpt.
- **Limitations:** Excerpt of the authors' own book. The original paper for the experiment was not located. One context (school board survey), one outcome, no statistics. The same 4.8% vs 2.7% figure appears in a publisher blog that was seen only in search results.
- **Implication for the skill:** Supports a default of shorter messages with fewer asks. Treat as a directional prior, not a calibrated effect size.

### C2. Harvard Gazette: interview with Todd Rogers on Writing for Busy Readers

- **Source:** Harvard Gazette (interview with Todd Rogers), 2023-10, <https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/10/tips-on-how-to-connect-with-people-who-dont-have-time-to-read>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** In a Harvard Gazette interview, Rogers and Lasky-Fink describe three of their six principles. (a) Less is more: cut words, ideas and requests, because each added request lowers the odds the top priority gets done. (b) Add structure: use headings and open with a paragraph explaining how to navigate the document, since readers jump around. (c) Use enough formatting, but no more: readers take bold, underline and highlight to mean this is most important; per the interview, mid-document emphasis increases reading of the emphasized section but decreases reading of the rest, and emphasis sharply increases how likely a section is to be read.
- **Limitations:** Interview with no sample sizes, effect sizes or outcome measures. The authors say dozens of randomized experiments support the six strategies but cite none in the article. Only three of six strategies are described. The authors themselves note tradeoffs ('there is no right amount to cut').
- **Implication for the skill:** Two testable formatting rules: at most one emphasis device per section, and a navigation paragraph for long documents. Evidence is the authors' claim, not independently verified.

### C3. Writing for Busy Readers (excerpt, 'When Writing for Busy Readers, Less Is More')

- **Source:** Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink (citing Gabrielle Adams et al.), unknown, <https://behavioralscientist.org/when-writing-for-busy-readers-less-is-more/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** The same excerpt reports an experiment by Gabrielle Adams and colleagues (University of Virginia): participants summarized a short article and then edited the summary to better capture its ideas. 83% added words rather than removing them. The excerpt says similar patterns appeared for other topics (travel itineraries, patents) but gives no figures.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The finding is a bias in editing toward adding, which matters most for the improve-existing-materials task.
- **Limitations:** Sample size not stated for the editing experiment. Single reported topic. Other-topic claims unquantified. The original Adams study was not located.
- **Implication for the skill:** When improving existing material, do not rely on the writer's instinct to cut. Enumerate candidate cuts sentence by sentence and require a keep or cut decision for each.

### C4. 2017 Letter to Shareholders (Amazon)

- **Source:** Amazon, 2018-04 (per secondary coverage; the letter covers fiscal 2017), <https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** firm-publication | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Amazon's 2017 Letter to Shareholders states that Amazon does not do PowerPoint or other slide-oriented presentations and instead writes narratively structured six-page memos. Each meeting opens with everyone silently reading one memo in what the letter calls a study hall. Memos carry no individual author ('the memo is from the whole team'). The letter says memo quality varies widely and that a strong six-page memo may take a week or more to write.
- **Limitations:** Company self-description of practice. No outcome data, no comparison with slide-based meetings, no evidence on decision quality.
- **Implication for the skill:** Narrative memos are a documented Amazon practice and can be cited as that practice. They are not evidence that memos produce better decisions.

### C5. Amazon's six page narrative structure

- **Source:** Shawn Callahan (Anecdote), 2018-05, <https://www.anecdote.com/2018/05/amazons-six-page-narrative-structure/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** A 2018 essay by Shawn Callahan (Anecdote) says the claim that Amazon's senior team banned PowerPoint has no direct source link, that most web commentary on the practice covers the meeting process rather than narrative structure, and that the 2004 Bezos email appears only as an image so its wording is unavailable in the essay. ### C6. Columbia Accident Investigation Board: The Boeing PowerPoint Slide (Tufte notebook)

- **Source:** Edward Tufte, quoting CAIB Final Report Vol. 1, 2003-08-26, <https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/columbia-accident-investigation-board-the-boeing-powerpoint-slide/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** expert-opinion | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Tufte's essay on the Columbia Accident Investigation Board (page dated August 26, 2003) quotes the board on the Boeing foam-impact briefing slide. The quoted CAIB passage says 'the choice of headings, arrangement of information, and size of bullets on the key chart' steered management toward what it already believed, and that uncertainties and warning signs were lost as information moved up the chain. The board describes 'the endemic use of PowerPoint briefing slides instead of technical papers' as an illustration of problematic technical communication at NASA. Tufte's own position: 'PP is inherently defective.' Commenters on the page argue that poor slides usually reflect unclear thinking or time pressure rather than the software.
- **Limitations:** A single case analysis, not a controlled test. CAIB passages are quoted via Tufte; the NASA report was not read directly. Tufte's tool-level claim is opinion, and commenters dispute it.
- **Implication for the skill:** Put uncertainty, risk and conditions in full sentences in the main text. A bulleted hierarchy can make a caveat look as minor as a footnote.

### C7. Garner, Alley et al. 2013, Effect of Slide Design on How Much the Audience Understands and Retains (IJEE 29(6)); conference abstract on assertion-evidence slides and comprehension

- **Source:** Joanna Garner, Michael Alley, and colleagues, 2013 (journal); conference version 2011 (per secondary), <https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/assertion-evidence-slides-appear-to-lead-to-better-comprehension--2/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Garner, Alley and colleagues compared topic-subtopic slides with assertion-evidence slides (sentence headline plus visual evidence) for a roughly six-minute narrated lecture on how MRI detects cancerous tumors. In the conference-version abstract, 55 participants saw topic-subtopic slides and 56 saw assertion-evidence slides. The assertion-evidence group scored higher on complex-concept questions, with some differences significant on an immediate essay and a one-week multiple-choice test. The topic-subtopic group viewed markedly more written text and did not score higher. The journal version (International Journal of Engineering Education 29(6), 2013; pages 1564-1579 per PDF metadata) is described in a teaching-center post as using an immediate multiple-choice quiz and a 17-item sentence-completion quiz one week later. That post gives no participant count and no statistics. A search summary reported p below .01, but this was not verified. The journal PDF could not be parsed.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The approach's developers ran the studies; treat the effect as plausible rather than established.
- **Limitations:** A single short lecture on one unfamiliar topic; novice students; one-week retention only; abstract-level reporting with no effect sizes. The two versions differ in participant numbers and outcome measures. The studies were run by the approach's developers. The journal full text was not read.
- **Implication for the skill:** Sentence headlines plus one visual have a plausible but modest evidence base. Prefer them as a default style, but do not state a quantified benefit for them in deliverables.

### C8. How the Design of Headlines in Presentation Slides Affects Audience Retention (Technical Communication 53(2):225-234); Farkas critique of the assertion-evidence approach

- **Source:** Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell, Muffo; critique by Farkas, 2006-05, <https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_57633847>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell and Muffo (2006) report, per catalog and abstract-level summaries, that sentence-style headlines stating a slide's central claim improved retention compared with phrase headlines. Per a secondary description, the trial ran in a lecture course with about 200 students per section, with two sections seeing phrase-headline slides and two seeing the sentence-headline versions over five class periods. The rewrite was accompanied by other changes, including typography and conversion of bullet lists to visual evidence. Separately, Farkas (University of Washington) is reported in a search summary (PDF not parsed) to find a good case for sentence titles because they focus attention on one idea, to call Alley's broader claims overstated and poorly supported, and to hold that brief, well-phrased bullet points do not harm a technical presentation.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The Farkas critique is the main counterweight in the literature found and should be carried into any rule about headlines.
- **Limitations:** Article text not read. Design is confounded because the headline rewrite came with other changes. No statistics seen. The Farkas critique is known only from a search summary.
- **Implication for the skill:** Sentence headlines are a low-cost default with plausible support. Do not write effect sizes into deliverables, and do not treat bullets as forbidden.

### C9. Verbal Redundancy in Multimedia Learning Environments: A Meta-Analysis (Adesope and Nesbit 2012); Yue, Bjork and Bjork 2013 (redundancy experiments)

- **Source:** Adesope and Nesbit; Yue, Bjork and Bjork, 2012 (Adesope and Nesbit); 2013 (Yue et al.), <https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** meta-analysis-or-review | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Adesope and Nesbit (2012, Journal of Educational Psychology; 57 independent studies, mostly postsecondary learners, per abstract summary) report that narration plus on-screen text performed about the same as text alone and better than narration alone. Spoken-written presentations beat spoken-only mainly for low prior knowledge learners, system-paced materials and picture-free materials. Keyword-style on-screen text (key terms from the narration) accounted for much of that advantage, rather than a word-for-word transcript. Yue, Bjork and Bjork (2013, Journal of Educational Psychology, doi 10.1037/a0031971) report two experiments with a narrated animation in which on-screen text identical to the narration reduced recall and transfer. A slightly reworded version did best, and heavy divergence was no better than duplication. Learners still preferred verbatim text.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Extrapolating to decks that repeat a memo word for word: no direct test was found, but the same mechanism (duplicate text competes with the reader's attention) is the proposed cause in the cited research.
- **Limitations:** Abstract-level only. Lab e-learning with mostly college students. Not tested with executive readers. Yue et al. is a pair of experiments. Clark and Mayer summaries seen in search are secondary.
- **Implication for the skill:** Slides should not repeat the speaker or the memo verbatim. Use keywords and short claims on screen.

### C10. Multimedia Design for Learning: An Overview of Reviews With Meta-Meta-Analysis

- **Source:** Noetel, Ginns, et al., 2021-10-23 (online); 2022 (print, Review of Educational Research 92(3), pp. 413-454), <https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** meta-analysis-or-review | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Noetel et al. (Review of Educational Research) synthesize 29 systematic reviews covering 1,189 studies and 78,177 participants, per abstract summary. They identify 11 multimedia design principles with significant positive effects on learning and five that significantly improve management of cognitive load. Signaling, temporal and spatial contiguity, and captioning of second-language video are named among the largest effects. Coherence (removing seductive details) and verbal redundancy have robust but not top-tier support. Design matters more for complex material and for system-paced settings such as lectures than for self-paced ones.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Applying these principles to executive documents and decks is an inference from learning studies, not a tested result.
- **Limitations:** Outcome is learning from instructional multimedia, not executive decisions. Per-principle effect sizes were not accessed. The full text returned HTTP 403 in this session. Signaling magnitudes conflict across sources (see disagreements).
- **Implication for the skill:** Use the principles as a diagnostic checklist: signal the structure, remove extraneous content, avoid redundant text, segment long content. Do not import numeric effect sizes. Presentations are the setting where design matters most, which supports a separate deck rule set.

### C11. Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect

- **Source:** Sundararajan and Adesope (first author listed as Sundararajan in the journal record), 2020-09, <https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** meta-analysis-or-review | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Sundararajan and Adesope (2020, Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect, Educational Psychology Review 32(3), pp. 707-734), per a secondary summary, synthesize 68 effects from 58 studies with more than 7,500 students. Interesting but irrelevant details reduced learning, with a small-to-moderate negative effect (g = -0.33). Harm was larger for static details, for text-and-image combinations, for material placed at the end of a lesson (a subgroup at g = -0.70), when learners could not control pace, and in print more than digital formats.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The meta-analysis is consistent with the coherence principle in multimedia learning, but its effects are measured on learning, not decisions.
- **Limitations:** Learning outcomes from instructional texts. Effect sizes come from a secondary summary; full text not read. Does not test executive or persuasive documents.
- **Implication for the skill:** Remove anecdotes, decorative visuals and tangential facts from decks and memos unless the deliverable is for engagement. The measured cost is in learning, not persuasion.

### C12. Structured methods summary box in a quantitative report: nonrandomized trial (JMIR Medical Informatics 10(4):e29813)

- **Source:** Koetsenruijter J, Wronski P, Ghosh S, Muller W, Wensing M, 2022, <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044155/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** A nonrandomized pilot (Koetsenruijter et al., 2022; 35 advanced medical students, not practicing decision-makers: control n=22, intervention n=13) tested a highlighted, bulleted summary box at the start of a methods section of a roughly 3,900-word report. The box drew attention from most participants. However, methods reading time was unchanged (4.03 vs 4.07 minutes; P=.96), understandability was unchanged (6.6 vs 6.6; P=.96), and perceived helpfulness for decision-making was lower in the box arm (2.9 vs 4.1; P=.09).
- **Limitations:** Small, exploratory pilot. The authors treated P below .1 as significant. Historical control group. Student rather than practicing-executive sample. Hypothetical scenario in a lab, in German. Time on the box does not confirm that the text was read.
- **Implication for the skill:** A summary box attracts attention without changing reading time or perceived helpfulness in this pilot. Put the answer in the opening prose of a document and do not use a box as a substitute. Test any boxed summary with readers before relying on it.

### C13. Describing and Comprehending Change in Quantitative Information (CogSci paper 0750)

- **Source:** Jie Gao and James E. Corter, unknown (conference page; logo alt text reads CogSci 2020), <https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0750/index.html>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Gao and Corter (Educational Testing Service and Columbia; CogSci conference paper 'Describing and Comprehending Change in Quantitative Information') report an experiment comparing chronological and reverse-chronological English sentences describing a change in a numeric quantity. Reverse-order text, which some news media use to lead with the ending value, required more processing time and produced less accurate answers to follow-up questions. The abstract says the experiment supported the hypothesis. The abstract gives no sample size, statistics or limitations.
- **Interpretation (agent):** A sentence-level rule with indirect evidence. It does not establish document-level order (bottom line first) effects.
- **Limitations:** Abstract-level only. Sentence-level task about numeric change, not whole documents. No sample size or statistics on the page. The full PDF was not read.
- **Implication for the skill:** When a number changes, state the starting value before the ending value in the same sentence. Avoid end-first constructions that lead with the new value and its comparison.

### C14. Plain Language: Writing for understanding (digital.gov, successor to plainlanguage.gov)

- **Source:** U.S. federal plain-language program (digital.gov), unknown, <https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** other | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Official plain-language guidance states that active voice makes it clear who should do what, and recommends present tense by default. It advises converting hidden verbs (nominalizations that often end in -ment, -tion, -sion or -ance, or follow verbs such as make, take or have) back into verbs, which usually shortens sentences. It lists shorter words and short sections as features of plain-language content. The page's legal basis is the Plain Writing Act of 2010, which requires public content to be written for its specific audience.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The guidance is clear and checkable at the sentence level; its effect on reader comprehension is not measured in the source.
- **Limitations:** Official guidance, not empirical testing. Confidence is high for what the guidance says, not for any effect on comprehension. The page does not cover organizing information, sentence-length targets, heading writing or lists; those are on linked pages not read.
- **Implication for the skill:** Enforce named actors as subjects, present tense by default, and verbs over noun forms. Make these checkable with a sentence-level pass.

### C16. BLUF (communication) (Wikipedia, search result only); AR 25-50 secondary copies

### C17. Pyramid Principle (Think Insights, secondary summary); SCQA descriptions from secondary sources

- **Source:** Barbara Minto (secondary descriptions), unknown (book 1985 or 1987 per conflicting secondary sources), <https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** expert-opinion | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Secondary descriptions of Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle give a top-down structure: one governing thought at the top, two to three supporting arguments beneath it, and evidence at the base. SCQA (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer) frames the opening. The method is credited to Minto's time at McKinsey. Sources conflict on the book's year (1985 in one secondary source, 1987 in a low-quality PDF). A vendor claim that it is the default at MBB firms is unverified.
- **Limitations:** No primary text accessed. Publisher and first-edition year not verified. No independent test of effectiveness found.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use the governing thought (one recommendation), two to three supporting arguments, and evidence below each. Use SCQA for an opening when the audience lacks context. Present as a structure, not as a proven effect.

## Disagreements

- **Sentence headlines versus topic headlines on slides:** Alley et al. (2006) and Garner et al. (2013) report better retention and comprehension with sentence headlines plus visuals. Farkas (search summary only) finds a good case for sentence titles but calls Alley's broader claims overstated and poorly supported, and says brief well-phrased bullets do not harm technical presentations. (C7, C8)
- **Participant counts and outcome measures for the assertion-evidence study:** The conference-version abstract reports 55 topic-subtopic and 56 assertion-evidence participants, with an immediate essay and a one-week multiple-choice test. The teaching-center summary of the IJEE journal version describes an immediate multiple-choice quiz and a 17-item one-week sentence-completion quiz and gives no participant count. A search summary reports p below .01 for the journal version; the fetched summary gives no statistics. (C7)
- **Whether on-screen text that is not verbatim helps:** Adesope and Nesbit (2012) find keyword-style on-screen text accounts for most of the benefit of spoken-written presentations. Yue et al. (2013) find verbatim duplication hurts, a slightly reworded version does best, and heavy divergence is no better than duplication. Both agree that verbatim duplication is not helpful; they differ on how much divergence is tolerable. (C9, C10)
- **Words-per-minute ceiling for on-slide text:** A Penn State assertion-evidence checklist (search summary) says audiences should read no more than about 20 words per minute. An older Penn State ASME/EYE guideline (search summary) says 15 words per minute. The two values are not reconciled in the sources seen. ()
- **Size of the signaling effect:** Noetel et al. (2021/2022) place signaling among the largest-effect principles (abstract summary, no number). A search summary of Richter et al. (2016) reports r = 0.17 for signals on transfer and comprehension, a small effect. A search summary of Schneider et al. (2018) reports d = .38, which was not verified. (C10)
- **Whether poor slides are caused by the tool or by the thinking:** Tufte (2003) says PowerPoint is inherently defective and that some methods are better independent of the user. Commenters on the same page argue that poor slides reflect unclear thinking or time pressure, not the software. The CAIB passages quoted by Tufte focus on how the format steered what management heard. (C6)
- **Summary boxes as a reading aid:** The intuition that a highlighted summary box helps readers, versus the Koetsenruijter pilot in which the box drew attention but did not change reading time, and perceived helpfulness was lower (P=.09). (C12)
- **Tension between cutting and adding structure:** Rogers and Lasky-Fink advise cutting words, ideas and requests, and also advise adding a navigation paragraph and headings to long documents. The authors concede there is no right amount to cut and that formatting involves competing effects. (C1, C2)
- **Length of the Amazon narrative memo:** Secondary sources describe a 2004 Bezos email referring to a four-page memo. Amazon's 2017 letter describes six-page narratives. The email text itself was not read, so the 2004 length claim is unverified. (C4, C5)

## Implementation recommendations

- [core] Open every document and deck with the answer: the recommendation or decision requested, in one or two full sentences, before background. (C13, C14, C16, C17). Consistent with the Pyramid structure (C17), official plain-language guidance (C14) and the BLUF doctrine (C16). Sentence-level evidence on order is indirect (C13). No randomized comparison of bottom-line-first vs chronological reports was found. Strength: moderate as a default structure, weak as a measured effect.
- [core] Make every slide title and section heading a full-sentence claim, not a topic label. Check by reading only the titles in sequence; they should carry the argument. (C7, C8). Sentence headlines have small, partly confounded support (C7, C8) with a credible critique (C8). The title-sequence check comes from practitioner guides, not from an accessed study. Strength: low to moderate.
- [core] One message per slide. If the title needs and to join two ideas, split the slide. (). Practitioner guidance seen only in search results. No accessed study tested it. Consistent with the assertion-evidence results (C7) but not directly tested there.
- [core] Do not put the speaker's script on slides. On-screen text should be short key terms or claims, not a sentence-by-sentence transcript of what will be said or what the memo already says. (C9, C10). Meta-analytic evidence that keyword text helps and verbatim duplication does not (C9), with an experiment showing verbatim duplication hurts (C9). Learning-study evidence, extrapolated to decks. Strength: moderate.
- [core] Remove seductive details from decks and memos: anecdotes, decorative visuals and tangential facts that do not bear on the decision, unless the deliverable is for engagement. (C10, C11). Meta-analysis shows interesting but irrelevant details reduce learning (C11), with the largest penalties when material is shown at the end and when pacing is not controlled. Strength: moderate in learning contexts; not tested with executives.
- [core] Keep asks and length tight: one primary request per message, and cut words, ideas and requests that do not change the decision. (C1, C2). A single reported field experiment shows a shorter invitation drew a higher response (C1). The authors report that each added request lowers the odds that the top priority is done (C2). Strength: low to moderate; no statistics in accessed sources.
- [core] When editing existing material for concision, make cuts explicit, item by item. Do not default to adding. Compare the revised text against a list of what must be kept. (C3). Reported experiment: 83% of participants added words when editing a summary (C3). Sample size not stated. Strength: low, but it is a cheap, testable process rule for the improvement task.
- [core] Limit emphasis: at most one emphasized element (bold, underline, highlight or colour) per section, reserved for the decision or a key number. (C2). Authors claim that mid-document emphasis draws reading to that section and reduces reading of the rest (C2). Evidence is the authors' claim with no effect sizes. Strength: low.
- [core] Run a plain-language sentence pass: named actor as subject, active voice, present tense by default, verbs instead of noun forms (-tion, -ment, -sion, -ance, or after make/take/have). (C14). Official guidance (C14). Strong as a checklist; no effect data found. Strength: guidance-level.
- [optional] When a number changes, state the starting value before the ending value in the same sentence, rather than leading with the new value. (C13). Single experiment on sentence order and numeric change (C13). Strength: low; sentence-level only. Interpretation, not a document-level rule.
- [core] State uncertainty, risk and conditions for the conclusion in full sentences in the main text, not only in bullets or footnotes. (C6). Investigative board observation that a bullet hierarchy can make caveats look minor (C6). Case evidence, not a controlled test. Strength: moderate as a cautionary case.
- [optional] For complex reasoning in a memo, use narrative prose that ranks and connects ideas. Use slides for data exhibits, not for arguments. (C4, C5). Amazon documents this practice (C4). The rationale is company and secondary commentary (C5). No public outcome evaluation. Strength: documented practice, unevaluated effect.
- [optional] Do not rely on a highlighted summary box in place of a top-line answer in prose. If a box is used, test it with readers. (C12).
