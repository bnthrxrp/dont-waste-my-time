## Findings

### AS1. Bain RAPID decision roles
- **Source:** Blenko, Mankins, Rogers, "Decisions: Who does what?", Bain & Company, 2011-05-04, <https://www.bain.com/insights/decisions-who-does-what/>. Accessed: yes. Practitioner article; no outcome data.
- **Finding:** Five roles. Recommend leads, gathers facts and proposes alternatives. Agree is a required sign-off (e.g. legal, regulatory). Input supplies data and judgment and has "the right to provide input to a recommendation but not to veto it". Decide is one person: "Giving the D to one individual ensures single-point accountability." Perform executes. The letter order is for memorability.
- **Implication:** For multi-stakeholder recommendations, name exactly one decision owner. List sign-off roles separately from input. Don't describe input-only contributors as holding a veto.

### AS2. Bain, "Make decisions work" (Decision Insights #3)
- **Source:** Blenko, Mankins, Rogers, Bain & Company, adapted from *Decide & Deliver* (HBR Press, 2010), 2010-12-28 (per fetch summary), <https://www.bain.com/insights/decision-insights-3-make-decisions-work>. Accessed: yes. Practitioner article; anecdotal examples only.
- **Finding:** To reset a troubled decision, clarify four elements:
  - **What:** state the decision explicitly; framing matters (Ford's reframed question).
  - **Who:** assign the roles.
  - **How:** one structured process, scaled to the value at stake.
  - **When:** deadlines ("Delay is the worst form of denial").
- **Implication:** A decision recommendation states the decision as a choice, then the alternatives considered, the decision owner, and a deadline or trigger.

### AS3. Bain, "The Five Steps to Better Decisions"
- **Source:** Bain brief, 2013-08-06 (per fetch summary), <https://www.bain.com/insights/the-five-steps-to-better-decisions/>. Accessed: yes.
- **Finding:**
  - Focus on the roughly 20-30 decisions that matter most, by value at stake.
  - Formality is warranted only where "the value of the decision justifies that kind of formality".
  - The brief reports a decision-effectiveness/performance correlation "at a minimum 95% confidence level" across 1,000+ companies, with **no method given**. Don't cite it.
- **Implication:** Scale decision structure to the stakes. Close each decision section by stating what was decided and why.

### AS4. Minto's own site (Minto Books International)
- **Source:** <https://barbaraminto.com/concept>, /course, /textbook and the home page. No date. Accessed: yes (summaries). Primary but promotional.
- **Finding:** "Extended thinking eventually ends in a single pyramid of ideas, at many levels, obeying logical rules, and held together by a single thought." The course covers how ideas "relate vertically and horizontally". The site brands "The Minto Pyramid Principle® and SCQ Framework℠". The terms "governing thought", "answer first" and "SCQA" were not found on the pages read.
- **Implication:** Use "SCQ" when attributing to Minto. Treat "governing thought / answer first / SCQA" as generic practitioner terms, not Minto quotes.

### AS5. McKinsey Alumni News profile of Barbara Minto
- **Source:** "Barbara Minto: 'MECE: I invented it, so I get to say how to pronounce it'", McKinsey & Company Alumni News, no date shown, <https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-insights/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it>. Accessed: yes.
- **Finding:** The profile quotes three rules:
  - "The point above has to be a summary of those below, because it is derived from them."
  - "You can't derive an idea from a grouping unless the ideas in the grouping are logically the same, and in logical order."
  - "The pyramid is a tool to help you find out what you think."
  
  Minto says she first abbreviated MECE and applied it to groups of ideas. Dates per the profile: joined McKinsey in 1963; book published in 1985.
- **Implication:** Two checks for every heading:
  - **Parent test:** the heading states what its children jointly prove. A topic label fails.
  - **Sibling test:** items are the same kind and in logical order (time, structure or importance).

### AS6. Lovallo & Sibony, "The Case for Behavioral Strategy" (excerpt copy)
- **Source:** McKinsey Quarterly, March 2010 (primary not read), accessed via a blog excerpt: <https://bobmorris.biz/?p=32640>. Secondary.
- **Finding:** A survey of 2,207 executives found only 28% said strategic-decision quality was generally good. A study of 1,048 major decisions asked how far 17 practices (8 analysis, the rest process) were applied; the data are self-reported. Conclusion: good analysis plus good judgment won't naturally yield good decisions without process. No effect sizes appear in the excerpt.
- **Implication:** Recommendations include a short "how we reached this" line: criteria, alternatives, dissent sought, where the analysis is thin. **Do not** cite the "six times" or "6.9 percentage points" figures that appear only in search snippets.

### AS7. MECE limitations (secondary summary)
- **Source:** Wikipedia, "MECE principle" (secondary), citing van Gelder (2010; primary returned 404) and Chevallier (*Strategic Thinking in Complex Problem Solving*, OUP 2016, p. 78; quote not verified).
- **Finding:** The critiques say MECE doesn't exclude irrelevant items, and that mutual exclusivity is sometimes undesirable because useful redundancy can exist.
- **Implication:** Issue-tree branches also pass a decision-relevance test ("does this branch change the answer?"). Prune branches that fail, and label any deliberate overlap.

## Disagreements and things to avoid
- Treat Bain's "Agree" as sign-off, not a veto.
- Minto's dates and SCQ vs SCQA: use the primaries (AS4, AS5).
- No primary source gives a "right number" of supporting points (secondary sources say 3, or 2-5). Don't encode a number as Minto's rule.
- Avoid:
  - "top consultancies use MECE issue trees as their primary tool" (uncited);
  - Bain's 95% correlation;
  - Lovallo & Sibony multipliers;
  - the McKinsey seven-step names and BCG storyline guidance as sourced method (not read).
