# Research dossier E (Layer 1, unvalidated)

**Research question.** How should business and executive materials be translated/adapted so meaning, numbers, terminology, tone and layout survive, with special attention to Brazilian Portuguese <-> English? Covers quality frameworks (ISO 17100, MQM), LLM/MT error modes and QA, CLDR locale conventions, PT->EN finance terminology with authoritative sources, text expansion, and translation vs localization vs transcreation.

## Key findings

### E1. ISO 17100:2015 - Translation services - Requirements for translation services (ISO catalogue page, plus secondary summaries)

- **Source:** International Organization for Standardization (ISO), 2015-05 (edition 1), <https://www.iso.org/standard/59149.html>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** ISO 17100:2015 (Translation services - Requirements for translation services) describes a workflow of translation with translator self-check, a mandatory second-person bilingual revision (source vs target), an optional monolingual review by a subject expert who need not be a translator, proofreading (sources differ on whether it is required), and a final verification by the project manager against agreed specifications. Translators are expected to raise doubts as queries rather than resolve them alone. The standard excludes raw machine translation plus post-editing and interpreting. The ISO catalogue shows Amendment 1 (2017), a second edition (ISO/AWI 17100) approved for development in July 2025, and a conflicting status entry.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The mandatory second-person bilingual revision is the control most likely to catch omitted or added content and wrong numbers, so the skill should require it when a human is available.
- **Limitations:** The ISO page returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch and the ANSI preview showed only the cover. Workflow details come from search snippets of vendor and certification blogs, not the normative text. The revision status is ambiguous across snippets (one says revision flagged August 2025, another says last confirmed 2020). Use only as the structural model for the procedure; verify the normative wording before quoting it.
- **Implication for the skill:** Structure the procedure as: translation, self-check, mandatory independent bilingual revision, monolingual target-language review, and a final PM-style check against a written spec. Do not treat a single translation pass as complete for executive materials.

### E2. MQM Pillars: Error typology (themqm.org); mirror at themqm.org/error-types-2/typology/

- **Source:** MQM Council / W3C MQM Community Group (community-maintained error typology), unknown (current site version), <https://themqm.org/mqm-pillars/typology/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** The MQM (Multidimensional Quality Metrics) typology's current top-level dimensions are: Terminology; Accuracy; Linguistic conventions (called Fluency in version 1); Style; Locale conventions; Audience appropriateness (called Verity in version 1); and Design and markup. Named Accuracy subtypes include Addition, Mistranslation and Omission. Under Mistranslation the page lists False friend, Misrepresentation of technical relationship and MT hallucination. The page recommends MQM Core as the default for comparability; the full typology contains further subtypes not shown on this page (for example Untranslated, which is not mentioned on the page).
- **Interpretation (agent):** Addition and Omission are the error classes most relevant to LLM output: they are where content is silently added or dropped, which is the risk for executive summaries that are shortened during translation.
- **Limitations:** The page has no subcategories listed for Terminology, Locale conventions or Fluency, and no severity definitions. The 'Untranslated' subtype was not verified. The older W3C community page lists a different and partly outdated set (including Internationalization and Other), so names differ by version.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use the seven current MQM dimensions as the error taxonomy in the QA checklist, with the named Accuracy subtypes (Addition, Omission, Mistranslation including false friends and MT hallucination).

### E3. The MQM Scoring Models (themqm.org)

- **Source:** MQM Council / W3C MQM Community Group, unknown, <https://themqm.org/mqm-pillars/the-mqm-scoring-models/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** The MQM scoring-models page defines four severity levels. Neutral: differs from preferred wording but acceptable. Minor: little disruption to use or understanding. Major: significantly undermines understanding, reliance or use, through a major change in meaning or an error in a highly visible part. Critical: makes content unfit for purpose or risks serious physical, financial or reputational harm; the page notes that in many systems 'a single Critical Error would automatically trigger a Fail Rating'. The page's sample severity multipliers are 0, 1, 5 and 25, with an exponential difference recommended and custom values allowed. Error type weights default to 1. Penalties are normalized per word and commonly scaled to 1,000 words. Samples of about 500 to 20,000 words are described.
- **Interpretation (agent):** A pass/fail gate with Critical = auto-fail fits decision documents better than an averaged score, because one wrong figure can mislead a decision regardless of the rest of the text.
- **Limitations:** The 0/1/5/25 multipliers are described as a sample, not a mandated scale. The raw and calibrated quality-score formulas are in images that were not readable in the fetched text. Normalization and calibration details are not required for an executive-document review.
- **Implication for the skill:** Map QA findings to MQM severities. Treat a wrong number, currency, date or period, or a fabricated or omitted caveat that changes a decision as Critical (automatic fail for an executive document). Treat a wrong financial term that changes meaning as Major. Treat style and fluency issues as Minor.

### E4. Findings of the WMT24 General Machine Translation Shared Task: The LLM Era Is Here but MT Is Not Solved Yet

- **Source:** Tom Kocmi et al. (WMT24 organizers and participants), 2024-11 (Proceedings of WMT 2024, Miami; DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.wmt-1.1), <https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.1.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** The WMT24 General MT shared-task overview (Kocmi et al.) evaluated outputs with professional annotators using Error Span Annotation (ESA). ESA asks annotators to mark error spans and each error's severity (Minor or Major), and to give a 0-100 score per segment, without classifying errors into a typology. The paper states that annotators were professional translators but not experts in MQM/ESA-style annotation. It reports that ESA produced 37% more clusters than DA+SQM while using only half the number of human annotations. The overview covered 11 language pairs, system outputs from 8 LLMs and 4 online providers, and test sets across 3 to 5 domains.
- **Interpretation (agent):** For this skill, the Minor/Major split plus an explicit Critical tier for numbers is a practical reviewer protocol derived from published practice.
- **Limitations:** Evaluation is for sentence- and paragraph-level MT in news, social, speech and other domains, not for business decks or memos. English-Portuguese was not one of the 11 pairs as far as the fetched text shows (not verified). The protocol differs from MQM, so severities are not directly comparable to MQM weights.
- **Implication for the skill:** A simple human-review protocol is supported: highlight each error span, tag it Minor or Major, and score the section. This is cheaper than full MQM typing and is adequate for a bilingual reviewer.

### E5. Findings of the WMT24 General Machine Translation Shared Task (sections 4.2, 7.2 and test-suite overview)

- **Source:** Tom Kocmi et al. (WMT24), 2024-11, <https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.1.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** WMT24 reports characteristic LLM output problems. The paper says LLMs produce verbose outputs, including an attempt to explain the translation or a refusal to translate, and that outputs not in the expected format were excluded from evaluation. A table note says the Gemini model refused to translate Icelandic. In the DFKI test suite for English-German, the paper says LLMs seem to perform worse than the two best NMT systems on punctuation, future verb tenses and stripping, and that some LLMs generate very verbose or empty outputs. The overview also states that the best system in general by human ranking was Claude-3.5-Sonnet, which won 9 language pairs.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The failure modes that matter for executive documents are silent omission and wrapper text that gets pasted into a deck, which is not caught by fluency checks.
- **Limitations:** Findings come from the WMT24 test sets and system versions of 2024; they are not measured on Brazilian Portuguese business documents. The phenomena list is specific to the test suite. Model versions have changed since.
- **Implication for the skill:** Before any LLM-produced translation is reviewed, check for preambles, explanations, refusals, truncation and empty output, and compare segment and paragraph counts against the source. These are omission and addition errors that are invisible if the reviewer reads only the target text.

### E6. Findings of the WMT24 General Machine Translation Shared Task (conclusions)

- **Source:** Tom Kocmi et al. (WMT24), 2024-11, <https://aclanthology.org/2024.wmt-1.1.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** In WMT24, the automatic-metric winner (Unbabel-Tower70B, best by automatic metrics) was not the winner across the board in human evaluation, and the paper concludes that 'human evaluation should be used as the final judge of translation quality'. The authors attribute the mismatch partly to metric bias in MBR decoding.
- **Interpretation (agent):** A high automatic score can coexist with a numeric or omission error that a bilingual human would catch.
- **Limitations:** Single shared-task evaluation; the ranking differences are for the test sets used. The paper does not give a general effect size for metric vs human disagreement in business text.
- **Implication for the skill:** Do not accept an automatic score (BLEU, COMET-type metric, LLM-judge score) as proof that a translation is faithful. Use it only for triage and keep a human bilingual reviewer as the final check.

### E7. Investigating Numerical Translation with Large Language Models

- **Source:** Wei Tang, Jiawei Yu, Yuang Li, Yanqing Zhao, Weidong Zhang, Wei Feng, Min Zhang, Hao Yang, 2025-01-09 (arXiv submission; accepted to ICASSP 2025 per page), <https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04927>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Tang et al. (2025) built a Chinese-English numerical translation test set from real business data covering ten numeric translation categories. They report that most open-source LLMs struggle; for large-unit terms such as 'million', 'billion' and the Chinese 'yi', even Llama 3.1 8B reaches error rates as high as 20%. The abstract says numerical translation errors are a common issue.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Scale words are where LLM translation of business numbers is most likely to be wrong, so the check should target them directly rather than rely on a general fluency review.
- **Limitations:** Abstract-level reading only; the full paper was not read. The study covers Chinese-English, not Portuguese-English, and reports no error breakdown by type in the abstract. Its mitigation strategies are named but their results were not in the fetched text.
- **Implication for the skill:** Make numeric fidelity a mandatory, mechanical check, with explicit attention to large-unit words and scale abbreviations (mil, milhão, bilhão; mi, bi; million, billion, K, M, B).

### E8. GEMBA-MQM: Detecting Translation Quality Error Spans with GPT-4

- **Source:** Tom Kocmi and Christian Federmann (Microsoft), 2023-10-21 (arXiv; accepted to WMT 2023), <https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13988>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** GEMBA-MQM (Kocmi and Federmann) uses GPT-4 with a fixed three-example prompt to mark translation error spans without reference translations, with language-agnostic prompts. The authors report state-of-the-art accuracy for system ranking in WMT23 and caution against using it to demonstrate improvements in academic work because it depends on a proprietary, black-box GPT model.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Using an LLM to highlight suspicious spans is cheap and useful as a first pass, but its misses are unknown for Portuguese business text, so human review remains required.
- **Limitations:** The abstract gives no per-language or per-error-type accuracy, and the fetched page does not state severity-level performance. It is a quality-estimation method for system ranking, not for document-level sign-off. Its results are for the GPT-4 version used in 2023.
- **Implication for the skill:** An LLM error-span judge can triage a translated document and flag candidate numeric or omission errors for a human to check, but it must not be the final sign-off.

### E9. Round-Trip Translation Reveals What Frontier Multilingual Benchmarks Miss

- **Source:** Ronald Skorobogat, Ameya Prabhu, Matthias Bethge, 2026-04-14 (arXiv submission), <https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12911>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Skorobogat, Prabhu and Bethge (2026) propose evaluating multilingual capability via round-trip translation: translate source to target and back, then treat meaning gaps as failures. They report round-trip scores correlate with LMArena user ratings at rho = 0.94, need no human reference translations, and do not need a judge more capable than the tested models. The abstract states no limitations of round-trip translation.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Back-translation catches gross drift but is weak on numbers, since a back-translated figure can look right even when the source-language figure was misread.
- **Limitations:** The correlation is at the level of model rankings across many languages, not validation of round-trip translation as a document-level QA check. Round-trip can hide errors that cancel out in the return trip. No primary study retrieved validates back-translation as a detector of numeric or omission errors in business documents. A vendor blog claims back-translation detects but does not improve translation; that claim was search-only and is not verified.
- **Implication for the skill:** Round-trip translation is a usable diagnostic for meaning drift on key claims and numbers, to be flagged for human review. It must not be presented as proof of fidelity.

### E10. Text size in translation (W3C Internationalization article)

- **Source:** W3C Internationalization (citing IBM Guidelines to design global solutions), unknown (undated page), <https://www.w3.org/International/articles/article-text-size/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** A W3C Internationalization article on text size in translation reproduces IBM guidance on English-to-European-language expansion by source length: up to 10 characters 200-300%; 11-20 characters 180-200%; 21-30 characters 160-180%; 31-50 characters 140-160%; 51-70 characters 151-170% (as printed); over 70 characters about 130%. The article says short labels are the main layout risk and recommends allowing text to reflow, avoiding small fixed-width containers, keeping abbreviations explained for translators, and planning database field widths for expansion.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Short KPI labels are the most exposed element in a dashboard translated from English to Portuguese, so the expansion budget matters most there.
- **Limitations:** The table is for English to European languages from IBM guidelines, not Portuguese specifically. The 51-70 row is printed as 151-170%, which looks anomalous. Expansion is not universal; the article notes some short terms stay the same length.
- **Implication for the skill:** In slides and dashboards, budget space for translated short labels (KPI titles, column headers, axis labels, button text) and let containers grow instead of fixing their width.

### E11. Search-level summary of translation text-expansion figures for Brazilian Portuguese (Kwintessential design guide and vendor/localization pages)

- **Source:** Various (vendor and localization blogs), unknown, <https://www.kwintessential.co.uk/blog/translation-text-expansion-how-it-affects-design-2>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Published figures for English-to-Brazilian-Portuguese text expansion disagree. One localization study reports about 13% growth (search-snippet level, method unverified); a language-specific app-store analysis reports about 30% average character expansion across 200 listings; a translation-design guide gives 15% to 30% and says technical texts usually expand less than literary texts; another vendor says 20-30%.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The sources agree that expansion is real and largest for short strings; the exact figure is not reliable enough to set in a template without a test.
- **Limitations:** The Kwintessential page fetch returned empty content, so the 15-30% range was not verified from the page. The other figures came only from search snippets. Measurement units differ (characters vs words; labels vs full sentences). No primary study of Brazilian Portuguese business slides was found.
- **Implication for the skill:** Plan around a 30% expansion headroom for short Portuguese labels when adapting English layouts, and run a visual overflow check rather than trusting any single percentage.

### E12. CLDR JSON numbers, dates and currencies for pt (Unicode CLDR)

- **Source:** Unicode Consortium (CLDR), unknown (current CLDR release on GitHub), <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unicode-org/cldr-json/main/cldr-json/cldr-numbers-full/main/pt/numbers.json>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** CLDR data for the Portuguese locale 'pt' (the locale whose data includes Brazilian conventions; pt_BR does not appear as a separate sublocale in the CLDR chart) gives: decimal separator ','; group separator '.'; decimal pattern '#,##0.###'; currency pattern '¤ #,##0.00'; percent pattern '#,##0%'. Compact short patterns: 1000 = '0 mil'; 1,000,000 = '0 mi'; 1,000,000,000 = '0 bi'; 1,000,000,000,000 = '0 tri'. Long compact patterns: '0 milhão' / '0 milhões'; '0 bilhão' / '0 bilhões'; '0 trilhão' / '0 trilhões'. Dates: short 'dd/MM/y'; long 'd 'de' MMMM 'de' y'. Time short 'HH:mm'. BRL symbol 'R$', displayName 'Real brasileiro'.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The pt compact scale (mi = 10^6, bi = 10^9, tri = 10^12) is the exact trap in PT->EN number translation: 'bi' means billion, not 'bilhão' in the English sense of trillion.
- **Limitations:** The pt file was read via a summarizing fetch; the exact space character in '¤ #,##0.00' was not confirmed as U+00A0 vs U+0020. That 'pt' represents pt-BR is inferred from the CLDR chart's sublocale list (pt_PT is listed, pt_BR is not). Compact patterns were read from the fetched file, not from the chart.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use these patterns as the pt-BR formatting reference: 1.234.567,89; R$ 1.234,56; 12,5%; 'R$ 2,3 mi', 'R$ 1,5 bi'; dates 29/10/2026 short and '29 de outubro de 2026' long.

### E13. CLDR JSON numbers and dates for en and pt-PT (Unicode CLDR)

- **Source:** Unicode Consortium (CLDR), unknown (current CLDR release on GitHub), <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unicode-org/cldr-json/main/cldr-json/cldr-numbers-full/main/en/numbers.json>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** CLDR English (en) numeric conventions: decimal '.', group ','; currency pattern '¤#,##0.00'; percent '#,##0%'; compact short '0K', '0M', '0B'; date short 'M/d/yy', medium 'MMM d, y', long 'MMMM d, y'; time short 'h:mm a'. CLDR Portuguese (pt-PT) numeric conventions differ from pt: decimal ','; group separator a space character; currency pattern '#,##0.00 ¤' (symbol after number); percent '#,##0%'.
- **Interpretation (agent):** A pt-PT document should not be used as the template for a Brazilian executive deck, because the currency and group conventions differ.
- **Limitations:** en-GB and en-US dates were not separately compared beyond en. The pt-PT group separator code point was not determined. The pt-PT data were read through a summarizing fetch.
- **Implication for the skill:** Converting a Brazilian figure to English requires changing the separators (1.234,56 -> 1,234.56), the scale words (mi/bi -> M/B or million/billion) and the currency symbol placement. Do not convert currency silently; R$ is BRL, not USD.

### E14. CPC 47 - Receita de Contrato com Cliente (CVM consolidated Rev. 14)

- **Source:** Comitê de Pronunciamentos Contábeis (CPC), consolidated by CVM, unknown (consolidated Rev. 14; linked by secondary sources to CVM Resolução 116, 2022), <https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/cvm_institucional/export/sites/cvm/menu/regulados/normascontabeis/cpc/CPC_47_Rev_14.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** The Brazilian text of CPC 47 (Receita de Contrato com Cliente, subtitled 'Correlação às Normas Internacionais de Contabilidade - IFRS 15') defines in Appendix A: 'Receita' as 'Aumento nos benefícios econômicos durante o período contábil...'; 'Preço da transação (para contrato com cliente)'; 'Obrigação de performance'; 'Ativo de contrato'; 'Passivo de contrato'; 'Cliente'; 'Contrato'. The standard uses 'faturamento' for the invoicing event, not for revenue, in its bill-and-hold guidance ('em alguns acordos onde há o faturamento, mas não há a entrega (bill-and-hold)'), and lists 'acordos do tipo “faturar e manter” (bill-and-hold)' in Appendix B. 'Receita' and its forms appear on dozens of lines; the English word 'revenue' does not appear.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Using 'faturamento' for 'revenue' in an English-to-Portuguese or Portuguese-to-English executive deck risks reporting billings or invoicing as income, which is a Critical-severity accuracy error.
- **Limitations:** The Portuguese text is the authoritative Brazilian rendering of IFRS 15, but the English equivalents of Portuguese terms are inferred from the IFRS 15 correspondence, not printed in the document. Some regulatory status (which CVM resolution is in force) was not verified.
- **Implication for the skill:** Translate 'revenue' as 'receita' (and 'revenue from contracts with customers' as 'receita de contrato com cliente') and flag 'faturamento' as a term that must be resolved by context rather than translated as revenue.

### E15. CPC 26 (R1) Apresentação das Demonstrações Contábeis (CVM consolidated Rev. 14)

- **Source:** Comitê de Pronunciamentos Contábeis (CPC), consolidated by CVM, unknown (consolidated Rev. 14), <https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/cvm_institucional/export/sites/cvm/menu/regulados/normascontabeis/cpc/CPC_26_R1_rev_14.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** CPC 26 (R1) item 7 defines 'resultado do período' as 'o total das receitas deduzido das despesas, exceto os itens reconhecidos como outros resultados abrangentes no patrimônio líquido'. Item 8 says the entity may use the term 'lucro líquido' to describe 'resultado', but recommends, for ease of communication, the closest possible adherence to the standard's own terms. The consolidated text uses 'Balanço patrimonial', 'Ativo circulante', 'Passivo circulante', 'Demonstração do resultado' and 'patrimônio líquido', and in the definition of owner it writes 'de capital próprio, no patrimônio líquido' in parentheses. Items 88-89 are headed 'Resultado líquido do período'.
- **Interpretation (agent):** 'Lucro líquido' is a permitted synonym in the standard, which is why management decks use it loosely; a reviewer should check whether a figure is the statutory total or a management net profit.
- **Limitations:** English equivalents (statement of financial position, current assets, profit or loss) are inferred from IAS 1 correspondence, not printed in the Portuguese text. Regulatory status of the consolidated text was not verified.
- **Implication for the skill:** For statutory-style lines, use 'resultado do período' / 'resultado líquido do período' translated as 'profit or loss for the period' or 'net income'. Reserve 'lucro líquido' for management commentary and keep the wording consistent within a document.

### E18. Translation vs localization vs transcreation (Globalization Partners and related search-level sources)

- **Source:** Various (vendor and academic), 2021-12-01, <https://www.globalizationpartners.com/2021/12/01/translation-transcreation-localization-are-they-the-same/>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Industry and academic glossaries distinguish translation (faithful rendering of meaning), localization (adapting content so it reads as native and adjusting currency, date formats and units), and transcreation (creative re-creation aimed at the persuasive or emotive effect, typically used in marketing). Sources differ on how sharp the boundaries are, one describing them as a spectrum rather than separate services, and the term transcreation is described as still being defined.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The executive adaptation is a practitioner recommendation consistent with these definitions; it is not a sourced standard.
- **Limitations:** Search-snippet level only; the GALA glossary entry was not retrieved. The sources are vendor and academic pages with no shared authority. No source was found that specifies when to provide a faithful translation plus a separate executive adaptation.
- **Implication for the skill:** Define three modes in the skill: faithful translation for record and legal use, localization for formats and units, and an executive adaptation (reordered, shortened, re-explained) labeled as an adaptation, not a translation.

## Disagreements

- **Revenue terminology: faturamento vs receita:** Practitioner and secondary usage (Suno explainer, search-level) treats 'faturamento' as gross sales or billings and links it to gross revenue, with net revenue as 'receita líquida'. The CPC 47 consolidated text (primary) uses 'receita' for revenue and uses 'faturamento' for the invoicing event (bill-and-hold). Both can be true across documents, so the skill should read the source's own usage before translating. (E14)
- **Vendor claim on equity and current-liability terms vs CPC text:** A translation-agency blog (search-level) says Brazil uses 'patrimônio líquido' and 'passivo circulante' while IFRS-aligned Portuguese uses 'capital próprio' and 'passivo corrente'. The CPC 26 (R1) text (primary) uses 'Passivo circulante' and 'patrimônio líquido', and writes 'capital próprio' only as a parenthetical gloss for owners' instruments. The blog's 'passivo corrente' usage is plausibly European Portuguese, which was not verified. (E15)
- **Portuguese text expansion magnitude:** About 13% (localization study, method unverified) vs 15-30% (translation-design guide; fetch returned empty) vs about 30% (app-store measurement of 200 listings, search-level) vs 20-30% (screenshot vendor). Differences probably reflect character vs word counting and short labels vs full sentences. (E11, E10)
- **MQM dimension names and count across versions:** The 2018-era W3C community issue list includes Internationalization and Other and mentions both 'eight' and 'seven' dimensions; the current MQM site lists seven dimensions with renamed Linguistic conventions (was Fluency) and Audience appropriateness (was Verity). (E2)
- **Severity schemes: WMT24 ESA vs MQM:** WMT24 ESA uses only Minor and Major plus a 0-100 score and no error typology. MQM uses four severity levels with penalty multipliers (sample 0/1/5/25) and an auto-fail for Critical. The schemes are not directly comparable. (E3, E4)
- **Human vs automatic quality judgments:** WMT24 shows the automatic-metric leader is not the human-ranking leader and concludes humans should be the final judge (E6). GEMBA-MQM proposes GPT-4 as an automatic error-span judge (E8) and cautions it is a black-box proprietary dependence. Round-trip translation shows a strong model-level correlation with user ratings (E9) without any document-level validation. (E6, E8, E9)
- **Back-translation as a QA control:** A vendor blog (search-level) says back-translation detects but does not improve translations and recommends third-party review. A 2024 review (secondhand summary, unverified) says it might be good enough when resources are limited but that no firm evidence shows best quality. No primary study of its detection rate for numbers or omissions in business text was retrieved. (E9)
- **ISO 17100 status:** One source says the standard was flagged for revision in August 2025; another says it was last reviewed and confirmed in 2020; ISO/AWI 17100 (a second edition) was approved for development in July 2025. The status is unresolved from the sources available. (E1)

## Implementation recommendations

- [core] Translation procedure for executive PT<->EN materials: (1) Build a terminology register first from the client's own documents and the CPC/IFRS texts for accounting terms; freeze it for the document. (2) Pre-extract every number, currency, percent, date, period label and unit from the source into a table. (3) Translate in sections, keeping the structure (headings, table rows, slide count). (4) Self-check the translator's own output against the register and the number table. (5) Independent bilingual revision: a second reader compares source and target segment by segment, marks errors with MQM dimension and Minor/Major/Critical severity. (6) Monolingual target-language review for style and fluency for the intended audience. (7) Numeric and entity check (section 3 below) and layout/overflow check (section 5 below). (8) Final sign-off against the original spec. If the audience differs from the source, deliver the faithful translation and a separately labeled executive adaptation. (E1, E2, E3, E4, E14, E15, E18). ISO 17100's workflow (translation, self-check, mandatory bilingual revision, monolingual review, final verification) is the accepted structure for translation services; the MQM and WMT24 sources support the severity-tagged review step; the register step addresses the terminology errors the CPC texts make visible.
- [core] QA checklist mapped to MQM dimensions and severities. Critical (auto-fail): any wrong number, currency code, percent, date, period or sign; a wrong unit or scale word; a fabricated or omitted caveat, risk or qualifier that changes a decision; wrapper text or refusal pasted into the deliverable. Major: a financial term that changes meaning ('faturamento' vs 'receita', 'resultado' vs 'lucro', 'orçado' vs 'previsto'); a false friend; omitted section, bullet, footnote or table row; text hidden or cut off by layout; an audience-inappropriate reference (for example a US-only program or regulation). Minor: fluency, grammar, punctuation, style or tone issues that do not change meaning. Neutral: preference-only wording. Mark each finding with its MQM dimension (Accuracy, Terminology, Linguistic conventions, Style, Locale conventions, Audience appropriateness, Design and markup). (E2, E3, E4, E14). The MQM dimensions and severity definitions are published; the auto-fail for Critical is stated on the MQM scoring page; the WMT24 Minor/Major protocol shows a lighter reviewer procedure is workable.
- [core] Numeric fidelity check (run on every translated document). (a) List every numeral, percent, currency symbol and code, scale word (mil, mi, bi, tri, milhão, bilhão, thousand, million, billion, K, M, B), unit, date and period label in source and target. (b) Normalize both sides to canonical values (for example 'R$ 2,3 mi' = 2,300,000 BRL; 'US$2.3M' = 2,300,000 USD) and compare. (c) Recompute every total, subtotal, percentage and percentage change in the target. Check whether a change is in percent or percentage points ('p.p.' = percentage points). (d) Check the currency code: R$ is BRL and must not become '$' or USD without an explicit, dated conversion stated in the document. (e) Check period labels: YTD ('acumulado do ano'), quarter, fiscal year vs calendar year, and month names. (f) Check negative and variance formatting (minus vs parentheses; favorable vs unfavorable labels). (g) Check numbers inside charts, axis labels, images and OCR text on slides. (h) Check that no figure was introduced, rounded or dropped silently by the translator or LLM. (i) Avoid numeric dates such as 03/04/2026 in executive text; write the month name. (E7, E12, E13, E3). LLM numeric errors concentrate in large-unit terms (E7); CLDR scale words mi/bi/tri differ from English M/B/T forms (E12, E13); MQM treats wrong figures as Critical (E3).
- [core] pt-BR formatting reference for Brazilian executive output: decimal comma, thousands dot (1.234.567,89); currency symbol before the number with a space ('R$ 1.234,56'); percent with no space before the sign ('12,5%'); compact forms 'mil', 'mi', 'bi', 'tri' (for example 'R$ 2,3 mi', 'R$ 1,5 bi', 'R$ 1 tri') with 'milhão/milhões', 'bilhão/bilhões', 'trilhão/trilhões' in full prose; short date 'dd/mm/aaaa'; long date '29 de outubro de 2026'; 24-hour time 'HH:mm'. en-US formatting reference for English executive output: comma thousands and point decimal ('1,234,567.89'); symbol before the number with no space ('$1,234.56'); percent with no space ('12.5%'); compact 'K', 'M', 'B' (for example '$2.3M', '$1.5B'); spell out month names in executive text; 12-hour time with am/pm. Never silently reformat a number across locales: when converting, convert separators, scale words and currency placement together, and keep a source-target table of key figures. pt-PT is not the template for Brazilian materials: it uses a space as group separator and places the currency symbol after the number. (E12, E13). These patterns come directly from CLDR pt, en and pt-PT data; the scale-word mapping is the main conversion trap.
- [core] Standard-sourced seed glossary (PT -> EN). Each entry is cited to a fetched primary text or marked inferred. receita -> revenue (CPC 47 App. A defines 'Receita'; English inferred from IFRS 15). receita de contrato com cliente -> revenue from contracts with customers (CPC 47 title). faturamento -> invoicing / billing (the invoicing event in CPC 47 bill-and-hold text); do not translate as revenue. faturar e manter -> bill-and-hold (CPC 47 App. B). preço da transação -> transaction price (CPC 47 App. A). obrigação de performance -> performance obligation (CPC 47 App. A). ativo de contrato -> contract asset (CPC 47 App. A). passivo de contrato -> contract liability (CPC 47 App. A). cliente -> customer (CPC 47 App. A). contraprestação -> consideration (CPC 47 text; English inferred). resultado do período -> profit or loss for the period (CPC 26 item 7; English inferred). resultado líquido do período -> profit or loss / net income (CPC 26 items 88-89). lucro líquido -> net profit in management text; statutory-line synonym for resultado per CPC 26 item 8, so check which is meant (CPC 26 item 8). patrimônio líquido -> equity (CPC 26 item 7; English inferred). capital próprio -> equity / owners' capital (appears as gloss in CPC 26). balanço patrimonial -> statement of financial position (CPC 26 table of contents; English inferred). demonstração do resultado -> statement of profit or loss (CPC 26; English inferred). ativo circulante / passivo circulante -> current assets / current liabilities (CPC 26 TOC; English inferred). regime de competência -> accrual basis (CPC 00 R2 para 1.17; English inferred). regime de caixa -> cash basis (practitioner; not verified in fetched text). mil / mi / bi / tri -> thousand / million / billion / trillion (CLDR pt short patterns). milhão / bilhão / trilhão -> million / billion / trillion (CLDR pt long patterns). R$ -> BRL (Brazilian real) (CLDR pt currency data). (E14, E15, E16, E12, E13). These entries have fetched primary-text support, which makes them defensible in a review; English equivalents marked inferred must be checked against the IFRS and IAS text before quoting.
- [reference] Practitioner-usage glossary entries (not verified in a primary source; confirm with the client before using in a deliverable). faturamento (management usage) -> gross sales or billings, depending on context; receita líquida -> net revenue; receita bruta -> gross revenue (tax-defined in some contexts); lucro bruto -> gross profit; margem de contribuição -> contribution margin; rentabilidade -> profitability (or return, depending on context); orçado -> budget (approved plan); realizado -> actual; meta -> target; previsto -> forecast or planned figure (decide by context); acumulado do ano -> year-to-date (YTD); inadimplência -> delinquency or default rate; ticket médio -> average ticket or average order value (define once); positivação -> active customers in the period (sales jargon; define once); sell-in / sell-out -> sell-in (shipments to channel) / sell-out (sales to end consumers), often left in English in Brazilian sales decks; giro de estoque -> inventory turnover. False friends to flag: atual ≠ actual; realizar ≠ realize (in the sense of understand). Each practitioner entry must be marked practitioner and listed in the document's terms note when used. (E14, E2). These terms are common in Brazilian management reporting but no primary source was retrieved for them; the skill should not present them as sourced.
- [core] Text expansion and layout rules for slides and dashboards. Budget about 30% extra width for short Portuguese labels (KPI titles, table headers, axis labels, buttons, tabs) relative to English, and test for overflow rather than relying on the figure. Let containers grow or wrap; avoid fixed-width tiles for text; keep short labels in their own rows where they sit beside form fields or inside graphics. Keep an abbreviation list for translators and do not invent new abbreviations in Portuguese without explaining them on first use. Check that aligned items stay aligned when they grow at different rates. Expansion is not universal; some short terms stay the same length, and some translations shrink, so leave room for both. (E10, E11). The W3C/IBM expansion table shows short strings expand most; the Portuguese figures disagree (13% to 30%) and are not from primary Portuguese studies, so a test-based margin is the defensible rule.
- [core] LLM output hygiene before review. Instruct the model to return only the translated text with no preface, explanation, notes or refusal. Reject any output that adds commentary, is empty, or is truncated. Compare segment, paragraph, bullet and table-row counts with the source. Compare the number table (section 3) before showing the result to a reviewer. For long documents, translate section by section and check each section, rather than one pass over the full text. (E5, E6). WMT24 reports verbose outputs, explanations, refusals and empty outputs from LLMs, and says output-format violations were excluded from evaluation.
- [reference] LLM judges and round-trip translation as triage only. An LLM error-span judge (GEMBA-MQM-style) may flag suspicious spans and numbers for a human to check. A back-translation pass may flag meaning drift in key claims and figures. Neither is a sign-off; neither is cited as evidence of fidelity in a deliverable. Use a different model or translator for the back-translation than for the forward translation where possible. (E8, E9, E6). GEMBA-MQM depends on a black-box model with uncertain coverage for Portuguese business text; round-trip evidence is model-level, not document-level, and no primary study validates it for business numbers.
- [reference] Translation vs adaptation labeling. When an executive audience differs from the source, produce (a) a faithful translation for records, legal or board-pack use, and (b) an executive adaptation that is shorter, reordered and re-explained, with its own label. Name the mode in the document's header or note. Do not present an adaptation as a translation. Localize units, date formats and currency per the locale rules; never convert currency without an explicit rate and date. (E18, E12, E13). Translation, localization and transcreation are defined in practitioner sources with blurred boundaries; the labeling rule keeps faithfulness auditable.
