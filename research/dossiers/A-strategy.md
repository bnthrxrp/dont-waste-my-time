# Research dossier A-strategy (Layer 1, unvalidated)

**Research question.** How do leading strategy firms and credible management literature structure problem-solving and executive recommendations, and what evidence and critique exists for these methods? Covers hypothesis-driven problem solving, issue and driver trees, MECE and its critiques, 80/20 prioritisation, so-what discipline, action titles and storyline-first working, decision-quality research (process vs analysis, alternatives, pre-mortems, decision rights, debiasing), and how recommendations should present alternatives, trade-offs, risks and the decision requested.

## Key findings

### A1. Before You Make That Big Decision... (Harvard Business Review, June 2011, reprint R1106B)

- **Source:** Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo, Olivier Sibony, 2011-06, <https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony argue that reviewers of a major proposal should examine the process that produced it as well as its content. They offer a 12-question checklist in three groups: questions decision makers ask themselves, questions to challenge the proposing team, and questions to evaluate the proposal. They say the screen should be applied by people independent of the recommending team, because people cannot reliably see their own biases.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The checklist can serve as a pre-send self-review for the author, not only as a critique tool for other people.
- **Limitations:** Read from a university-hosted reprint PDF of the HBR article. The canonical hbr.org page (hbr.org/2011/06/the-big-idea-before-you-make-that-big-decision) showed only the introduction before a paywall. The article describes the checklist but reports no validation of its effect on decision outcomes.
- **Implication for the skill:** Give the skill a reviewer-style checklist for recommendations (alternatives, dissent, provenance of numbers, optimism, worst case, self-interest) and apply it to how the recommendation was produced, not only to its text.

### A2. Before You Make That Big Decision... (Harvard Business Review, June 2011)

- **Source:** Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo, Olivier Sibony, 2011-06, <https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Question 5 asks whether credible alternatives were considered. The article says a good process evaluates other alternatives objectively and warns that individuals and groups tend to generate one plausible hypothesis and then seek only evidence that supports it. It recommends insisting that proposers submit at least one or two alternatives to the main recommendation with their pros and cons, and asking at what stage alternatives were discarded and whether the team looked for evidence that could disprove its main hypothesis. It also warns that some proposals include a perfunctory list of risks or implausible alternatives that make the recommendation look good by contrast.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The perfunctory-risks warning is a direct instruction to avoid risk tables used as decoration in recommendation documents.
- **Limitations:** Same reprint as A1. The checklist is the authors' own design; the article gives no outcome data for it.
- **Implication for the skill:** Require at least one or two genuine alternatives, each with pros, cons and the reason it was rejected. Reject strawman options. Include a step that states what evidence would disprove the lead hypothesis.

### A3. Before You Make That Big Decision... (Harvard Business Review, June 2011)

- **Source:** Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo, Olivier Sibony, 2011-06, <https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Question 10 says most recommendations contain forecasts prone to excessive optimism, and that correcting this requires an outside view (a statistical comparison with similar past projects) rather than a bottom-up inside view of the case at hand, which the authors say tends to underestimate. Question 11 states that the worst case is rarely bad enough. It asks where the worst case came from, how sensitive it is to competitors' responses, and what has not been thought of. The authors suggest a pre-mortem to surface a worst case and war games to model competitor reactions.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Base-case optimism and thin worst cases are the two most checkable failure signals in a recommendation.
- **Limitations:** The claim that inside-view estimates underestimate is the authors' assertion, not a measured result within the article.
- **Implication for the skill:** Ask for a reference-class comparison behind any base-case timeline or revenue figure, a worst case with a stated origin, and an explicit competitor-response line.

### A4. Before You Make That Big Decision... (Harvard Business Review, June 2011)

- **Source:** Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo, Olivier Sibony, 2011-06, <https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** The article says its checklist is not designed for routine decisions that an executive formally rubber-stamps. It says the sweet spot for quality control is decisions that are both important and recurring, which justify a formal process. Its own examples nonetheless apply the checklist to a large acquisition, which is a one-off decision, so the boundary is stated as a guideline rather than a sharp rule.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The article supports proportionality: the full review is for decisions where a bad call is costly, not for every item that reaches an executive.
- **Limitations:** Internal tension between the important-and-recurring sweet spot and the one-off acquisition example. The article gives no threshold for stakes.
- **Implication for the skill:** Lightest-adequate rule: run the full review only when a decision is consequential or irreversible or recurs across units. For routine or rubber-stamped decisions, use a short decision statement instead.

### A5. Before You Make That Big Decision... (Harvard Business Review, June 2011), citing The Case for Behavioral Strategy (McKinsey Quarterly, March 2010)

- **Source:** Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo, Olivier Sibony (citing Lovallo and Sibony, McKinsey Quarterly), 2010-03 (McKinsey Quarterly, per the HBR citation; original not accessed), <https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** The HBR article cites a recent McKinsey study of more than 1,000 major business investments. It says organizations that worked at reducing the effect of bias in their decision-making processes achieved returns up to seven percentage points higher, and points to Lovallo and Sibony, The Case for Behavioral Strategy (McKinsey Quarterly, March 2010), for more detail.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The figure is a motivating claim from the authors' own firm and cannot be checked from the sources read.
- **Limitations:** Accessed only as the HBR citation. The original McKinsey article returned HTTP 503 on every attempt, so sample design, outcome definition and causal identification are unknown. The authors are McKinsey-affiliated. A search snippet paraphrased the figure as a flat seven points; the HBR wording is up to seven points, which matters.
- **Implication for the skill:** Do not quote the seven-point figure in skill output without the up to qualifier and a source. Treat it as motivation, not evidence of effect size.

### A6. Performing a Project Premortem (Harvard Business Review, September 2007, reprint F0709A)

- **Source:** Gary Klein, 2007-09, <https://comprobo24.github.io/assignments/performing_a_project_premortem.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Klein describes a premortem. At the start of a project the team assumes the plan has failed. Each member independently writes every plausible reason for the failure, reasons are read out one per person in turn, and the project manager reviews the list to strengthen the plan. The rationale is that people with reservations often stay quiet during planning, and the method makes it safe for knowledgeable skeptics to speak. Klein reports that a 1989 study by Mitchell, Russo and Pennington found prospective hindsight (imagining that an event has already occurred) increased the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%. The article's support is three anecdotal session examples, with no comparative outcome data.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Use the premortem for the reasons it surfaces, not as evidence that the project will succeed.
- **Limitations:** Read from a reprint copy hosted outside HBR; the hbr.org page showed only the summary and introduction before a paywall. The method description is reliable; the effectiveness evidence is anecdotal. The 30% figure is Klein's report of a 1989 study measuring the reasons people generate, not a measure of project success. I did not read the 1989 paper.
- **Implication for the skill:** Add a premortem block to recommendations: state that the plan has failed and list 3 to 7 plausible causes, each with a mitigation and an owner.

### A7. Making fast strategic decisions in high-velocity environments (Academy of Management Journal, 1989, 32(3): 543-576; reprinted in Strategy as Practice, Cambridge, chapter published online 2010-03-10)

- **Source:** Kathleen M. Eisenhardt, 1989, <https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/strategy-as-practice/making-fast-strategic-decisions-in-highvelocity-environments/4397236CC78572C22EA205AE1E6B7795>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Eisenhardt's inductive study of eight microcomputer firms in a high-velocity industry reports that fast strategic decision makers develop more alternatives, not fewer, and use more information, not less. Speedy teams use a two-tiered advice process. Resolving conflict and integrating strategic decisions with tactical plans are critical to speed. Fast decisions based on this pattern are linked to superior performance. The chapter editors describe these findings as sharply contrary to the views on rapid decision making current when the paper appeared.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Speed and breadth are compatible when teams run options in parallel and resolve conflict quickly.
- **Limitations:** I read the Cambridge abstract and editors' summary only. The full paper was not readable: the Cornell post-print is a scanned PDF with no text layer and no OCR tool was available. Eight firms, inductive design, propositions rather than hypothesis tests. A search summary (unverified) says the model has not been tested on a larger sample.
- **Implication for the skill:** Supports generating several options in parallel and gathering more data early, rather than narrowing to one option to save time. Breadth and speed are not opposed in this evidence.

### A8. Surprising but true: Half the decisions in organizations fail (Academy of Management Executive, 1999, 13(4): 75-89)

- **Source:** Paul C. Nutt, 1999, <https://cebma.org/assets/Uploads/Nutt-1999-gecomprimeerd.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Nutt studied 356 decisions made by senior managers in medium-to-large US and Canadian organizations (about a quarter in public agencies, about half in private profit-making firms). He reports that tactics prone to failure were used in two of every three decisions, and that failures trace to managers who impose solutions, limit the search for alternatives and use power to implement. The most common way of identifying options was adopting an existing solution (223 of 356 decisions, about 63%), with sustained use of 55% and full use of 41% in Table 4. Intervention, the least common direction-setting tactic (about 7% of cases), had sustained use of 96% and full use of 92%. The paper says results do not vary much across decision types or organizations.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The headline half-fail figure depends on the paper's implementation-based success measures, so it should be quoted with that definition.
- **Limitations:** Retrospective and observational. Success is measured by sustained use (two years) and degree of full use, so failure is an implementation-based definition. Table values were read from OCR of a PDF copy and should be checked before quoting. Sample selection is not clear from the part I read.
- **Implication for the skill:** Set explicit objectives, search for several options including benchmarks, and involve the people who must implement. Do not present a single pre-chosen solution as if it were the decision.

### A9. Does Decision Process Matter? A Study of Strategic Decision-Making Effectiveness (Academy of Management Journal, 1996, 39(2): 368-396)

- **Source:** J. W. Dean and M. P. Sharfman, 1996, <https://doi.org/10.5465/256784>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Dean and Sharfman's 1996 longitudinal field study of 52 strategic decisions in 24 companies tested whether procedural rationality and political behaviour affected decision success, controlling for environmental favourability and implementation. The authors conclude that the way decisions are made bears on whether they succeed.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Corroborates the process-over-content theme of A1 and A8, but this source was not read in full.
- **Limitations:** Accessed only through abstract-level search results; the JSTOR and AOM pages returned 403 or 404. Effect sizes, measures and the direction of the procedural-rationality result are not verified. Citation counts differ across indexes.
- **Implication for the skill:** Supports the general claim that decision process matters. Use only with the caveat that the direction and size of the effect are unverified.

### A10. How do you define a good decision? (CBS News interview with Carl Spetzler)

- **Source:** Carl Spetzler, unknown (page shows Updated on May 5, 2009), <https://www.cbsnews.com/news/carl-spetzler-the-six-elements-of-a-good-decision>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** expert-opinion | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Spetzler lists six elements of a good decision: the right frame (solving the right problem), clarity about what is wanted, creative alternatives, the right information including uncertainty, sound reasoning that accounts for what is known and unknown, and commitment to follow through. He says a decision is only as strong as its weakest link and that teams usually fall short on all six. In workshops he asks groups to trace significant past decision failures to the weakest element.
- **Interpretation (agent):** A checklist of six elements gives a practical end-of-draft test that covers framing as well as analysis.
- **Limitations:** A short news interview. The book Decision Quality (Spetzler, Winter, Meyer, Wiley, 2016) could not be read (O'Reilly preview returned 403), so its wording is from search summaries only. The claim that teams fall short on all six is Spetzler's observation from workshops, not a measured result.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use the six elements as a final self-check before a recommendation goes out. The weakest element caps the quality of the whole, so a strong analysis cannot rescue a weak frame or missing alternatives.

### A11. Great Decisions, Not a Solo Performance (Bain & Company Insights)

- **Source:** Anna Cochemé, Ludovica Mottura (Bain & Company), 2026-10-07 (as shown on the page), <https://bain.com/insights/decision-insights-10-great-decisions-not-a-solo-performance>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** firm-publication | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Bain's article (by Anna Cochemé and Ludovica Mottura, published October 7, 2026, per the page) describes RAPID roles. Recommend: does most of the groundwork (the article estimates about 80%), gathers input, proposes a course of action and coordinates the other roles; each decision should have only one R. Agree: confirms requirements such as legal or regulatory rules; concerns must be resolved before the decision, but Agree cannot veto, and unresolved disagreements go to the decision maker. Perform: carries out the decision, assigned promptly. Input: advisory expertise and data; the recommender need not reflect every input. Decide: makes the final call and is the single point of accountability, including resolving deadlocks between R and A. The article recommends one decision maker per decision, with any group agreeing in advance how it will decide, and places the D at a level close enough to implementation.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The Agree role is advisory with a resolution requirement, not a veto, so a recommendation can proceed with unresolved concerns that are escalated to the decision maker.
- **Limitations:** The page was read through a summarising fetch. The 80% figure and role wording should be spot-checked against the page. Third-party sites describe the Agree role differently (see disagreements).
- **Implication for the skill:** When a recommendation involves several parties, name who recommends, who decides (a single D), who must agree (conditions resolved before the decision), who performs, and who only provides input.

### A12. MECE principle (Wikipedia)

- **Source:** Wikipedia contributors (secondary; critics named within), unknown, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECE_principle>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** The Wikipedia summary of MECE criticisms lists three. First, MECE tests distinctness and coverage but not relevance, so a list can pass both tests and still include items that do not belong (citing Tim van Gelder, 2010). Second, requiring the answers themselves to be mutually exclusive can be needlessly constraining (cited to an undated web page). Third, excluding overlap rules out redundancy that is sometimes necessary (citing Arnaud Chevallier, Strategic Thinking in Complex Problem Solving, OUP 2016, p. 78). The article attributes MECE to Barbara Minto, who developed it at McKinsey in the late 1960s.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The strongest critique is that MECE is necessary but not sufficient: relevance is a separate test.
- **Limitations:** Secondary. The van Gelder essay could not be fetched (404), so its argument comes from a search excerpt only. The Chevallier citation was not checked. I found no empirical study testing whether MECE improves decisions or deliverables; the critiques are conceptual.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use MECE as a check for completeness and relevance, not as a mandatory structure. Allow overlapping groups when that makes the argument clearer, and check that every item belongs.

### A13. The pyramid principle (Stratrix craft page)

- **Source:** Stratrix (vendor practitioner page; no byline), unknown (footer shows 2026; no byline or date), <https://stratrix.com/craft/pyramid-principle>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** A practitioner page on the pyramid principle says the governing point goes first and each level supports the level above; the hard part is the joints, where each summary point must state what the ideas beneath it mean. It says a heading that names a topic is a drawer and a heading that makes a claim is an argument, and offers four tests for summary points (so-what, derivation, question, same-kind). It describes a 22-slide pricing review with the recommendation on the last slide. Moving the recommendation to slide one without rewriting topic headings left the CFO asking so what.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Moving the answer to the front is not enough; the supporting headings must be rewritten as claims too.
- **Limitations:** Vendor practitioner page with no evidence of effect. The pricing example is illustrative and the page does not say it is a real case. The page does not define action titles or ghost decks.
- **Implication for the skill:** Write every heading as a claim that the evidence beneath it proves, and run the so-what test on each. Topic labels fail. Put the answer first, then rewrite headings so they still carry the argument.

### A14. Bulletproof problem solving (McKinsey & Company insight page and podcast transcript; book by Charles Conn and Robert McLean, 2018)

- **Source:** McKinsey & Company (Rob McLean; Charles Conn), unknown (podcast transcript; search summary says about seven years old), <https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/want-better-strategies-become-a-bulletproof-problem-solver>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** Search excerpts of a McKinsey podcast with Rob McLean, tied to the Conn and McLean book on bulletproof problem solving, describe bulletproof as work that holds up to client scrutiny, judged by how rigorously the problem was defined, broken down and analysed. They recommend forming hypotheses and testing them against simple data before running heavier algorithms, and describe logic trees for breaking a problem into parts and prioritising branches. A McKinsey alumni piece describes putting strong hypotheses on the table early and refining them through testing.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Hypothesis-first is the practitioner default; the evidence for it is practice-based, not outcome-based.
- **Limitations:** The McKinsey page returned HTTP 503 on repeated attempts, so these claims come from search excerpts only. The seven-step summary is from a McKinsey newsletter that was not opened.
- **Implication for the skill:** Hypothesis-first with issue trees is the default practice of leading firms. Pair it with the disconfirmation step in A2 so early hypotheses do not harden into confirmation.

### A15. Scenario Planning: A Tool for Strategic Thinking (MIT Sloan Management Review, Winter 1995, 36(2))

- **Source:** Paul J. H. Schoemaker, 1995-01-15 (as shown on the page), <https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/scenario-planning-a-tool-for-strategic-thinking/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** expert-opinion | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Schoemaker proposes scenario planning as a way to counter overconfidence and tunnel vision. A ten-step process runs from setting scope and time frame, through mapping stakeholders, trends and key uncertainties, to drafting scenario themes, testing them for internal consistency and plausibility, and converging on decision scenarios. In a small experiment with 68 evening MBA students, confidence ranges widened by about 50% on average after scenario construction, while best estimates changed less; the author reports similar effects whether the participant or someone else built the scenarios. Support is mainly case examples (such as Shell and Anglo-American) and that one experiment. The article warns that vivid, causally coherent scenarios can seem more probable than they are.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The method's value is in widening the range of estimates; the 50% effect size itself is weak evidence.
- **Limitations:** The 68-student figure and the 50% widening come from a summarising fetch of the page; verify them in the article. The experiment is a single small study at one university. Most support is case-based.
- **Implication for the skill:** For uncertain-market recommendations, present three or four scenarios that vary several uncertainties together, report the range, and label scenarios as plausible rather than likely.

### A16. Pareto principle (Wikipedia)

- **Source:** Wikipedia contributors (secondary), unknown, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** secondary-summary | **Confidence:** low
- **Finding:** The Wikipedia page on the Pareto principle describes 80:20 as shorthand and a rule of thumb. Real splits vary (for example 90:10 or 70:30), and the pattern corresponds to power-law distributions. Juran applied the pattern to quality control in 1941 and later preferred the vital few and the useful many, so the remaining 80% is not treated as worthless. The page's support is a mix of examples and models, several claims carry citation-needed tags, and it cautions that Pareto analysis can overlook small problems that grow over time.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The 80/20 split is a heuristic to test in the data, not a law to assume.
- **Limitations:** Secondary and partly uncited. I found no rigorous test of whether acting on the vital few improves outcomes.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use 80/20 as a prioritisation hypothesis to check against the actual distribution, not as a fixed ratio to assert. Do not silently drop the long tail.

## Disagreements

- **Role of Agree in RAPID:** Bain (primary, A11): Agree confirms requirements and its concerns must be resolved before the decision, but it cannot veto. Umbrex (third-party, search excerpt): Agree is a narrow veto exercisable only when predefined thresholds are breached. Bridgespan nonprofit guide (search excerpt): Agree is input the recommendation must account for, limited to each person's expertise. Resolution: use Bain's wording as the primary source. (A11)
- **Hypothesis-first work versus the single-hypothesis trap:** McKinsey practice (A14, search excerpts): put strong hypotheses on the table early and test them. Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony (A2): people generate one plausible hypothesis and seek only supporting evidence. These are not strictly contradictory; the resolution used here is to pair an early hypothesis with an explicit disconfirmation step. (A14, A2)
- **MECE as norm versus as rigid constraint:** Practitioner norm: consulting reviewers flag slides as not MECE (search excerpt, practitioner, unverified). Critics: MECE does not ensure relevance (van Gelder, via A12), can be needlessly constraining when applied to answers, and can exclude useful redundancy. No empirical study found to settle it; the disagreement is conceptual. (A12)
- **Size of the bias-reduction return:** HBR text (A5): returns up to seven percentage points higher. Search snippet of the McKinsey article: raised returns by seven percentage points (flat). The qualifier matters; use the HBR wording. Original McKinsey article not accessed. (A5)
- **Scope of the review checklist:** Article's guideline (A4): sweet spot is decisions that are important and recurring; routine rubber-stamp decisions excluded. Article's own examples (A1): the checklist is applied to a large one-off acquisition. The boundary is a guideline, not a precise rule. (A4, A1)
- **Speed versus breadth of alternatives and information:** Conventional view of the 1989 period, as the chapter editors describe it: fewer options and less information speed decisions. Eisenhardt (A7): fast decision makers develop more alternatives and use more information. Nutt (A8): narrow search and imposed solutions go with failure. Sources agree in direction; the disagreement is with the prevailing view. (A7, A8)

## Implementation recommendations

- [core] Scale the method to stakes: run the full review (alternatives, dissent check, outside-view forecast, worst case, premortem) for consequential, irreversible or recurring decisions; for routine decisions, use a short decision statement with the ask and one alternative. (A4, A1). The HBR authors say the checklist is not designed for routine decisions an executive rubber-stamps and that the sweet spot is important, recurring decisions. Heavy process on routine items wastes the reader's time.
- [core] Every recommendation shows at least two genuine alternatives, each with pros, cons and the stage and reason it was rejected. A single-option recommendation must state why no alternative is credible. (A2, A8, A7). The article puts the alternatives question first in its process review and warns about strawman options. Nutt links narrow search and imposed solutions to failure; Eisenhardt links more alternatives to faster, better decisions.
- [core] Anchor the base case to an outside view (comparable past projects or a named reference class). Give a worst case with a stated origin and a line on competitor response. (A3, A15). The article says base cases are prone to optimism and that the worst case is rarely bad enough. Schoemaker shows that ranges widen when multiple uncertainties vary together.
- [core] Add a premortem block: state that the plan has failed and list 3 to 7 plausible causes, each with a mitigation and an owner. Avoid a perfunctory risk table. (A6, A3, A2). Klein's method, which the HBR authors also recommend. The article warns against perfunctory risk lists that make the recommendation look good by contrast.
- [core] When several parties are involved, name the roles: one Recommend, one Decide, Agree conditions resolved before the decision, Input advisory, and a named Perform owner. (A11). Bain's RAPID framework requires a single decision owner and states that Agree cannot veto. Ambiguous roles are a common reason decisions stall.
- [core] Write headings as claims that the evidence beneath proves, and run the so-what test on each. Topic-only headings are not acceptable in a recommendation. Put the answer and the ask first. (A13, A14). Stratrix (practitioner) describes topic headings as drawers that leave the thinking to the reader and fails on the so-what test. The evidence here is practitioner-level.
- [core] State the lead hypothesis and the evidence that would disprove it, and show that the disconfirming evidence was checked. (A2, A14). Kahneman et al. warn that people seek only supporting evidence; McKinsey practice is hypothesis-first. Pairing the two avoids confirmation bias in issue-tree work.
- [core] Include a short how this was produced note: alternatives considered, dissent and how it was handled, key assumptions and sources. (A1, A8, A9). The article's central recommendation is to review the process behind a recommendation, not only its content. Nutt ties decision process to success; Dean and Sharfman support this but were not read in full.
- [reference] Use MECE as a completeness and relevance check, not a mandatory structure. Allow overlapping groups where they make the argument clearer, and remove items that do not belong. (A12). Critics find MECE does not ensure relevance and can exclude useful redundancy. No empirical support for rigid use was found, and practitioners treat it as a norm.
- [reference] Run a six-element self-check before finalising a recommendation: frame, objectives, alternatives, information and uncertainty, reasoning, commitment. Fix the weakest element first. (A10). Spetzler's weakest-link argument. The source is an interview; the book's wording was not read.
- [optional] Treat 80/20 as a hypothesis: check the actual concentration in the data before asserting it, and keep the long tail visible rather than dropping it. (A16). The split is an approximation tied to power-law distributions, not a law. No rigorous test of the vital-few approach was found.
- [optional] For uncertain markets, offer three or four scenarios that vary several uncertainties together, report the range, and label scenarios as plausible rather than likely. (A15). Schoemaker reports wider confidence ranges after scenario construction in a small study, and warns that vivid scenarios can seem more probable than they are.
- [reference] Do not quote the McKinsey seven-percentage-point return figure, the Klein 30% figure or the Schoemaker 50% figure without the qualifier and source. (A5, A6, A15). Each comes from a secondary citation or summary, and the McKinsey original could not be read. The HBR wording is up to seven points.
