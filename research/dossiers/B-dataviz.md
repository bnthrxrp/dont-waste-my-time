# Research dossier B-dataviz (Layer 1, unvalidated)

**Research question.** What evidence-based rules should govern chart selection, encoding, labeling, color and honesty in executive materials? Covers graphical perception rankings, axis truncation and deception, pie/donut evidence, embellishment and memorability, titles and captions, color and accessibility (WCAG, color vision deficiency, palettes), uncertainty visualization, IBCS notation, and small multiples versus overlays.

## Key findings

### B1. Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception: Using Mechanical Turk to Assess Visualization Design

- **Source:** Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock, Stanford University (CHI 2010), 2010-04, <https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Heer & Bostock replicated Cleveland & McGill's position-length experiment (Exp. 1A, proportional judgment) on Amazon Mechanical Turk, covering position on a common scale, position on non-aligned scales, length, angle (as pie) and circular area (as bubble). The accuracy ranking of judgment types matched the original study. Position encoding still significantly outperformed length encoding, although length judgments were more accurate than in the original study. The authors say the crowd results match prior lab work and are consistent with theoretical predictions, and conclude crowdsourcing is viable for graphical perception research. Setup: N=50 subjects per chart, 70 HITs, USD 0.05 per judgment; only 14 of 3,481 responses to the verification question were incorrect (0.4%). The paper's background also notes that background luminance, plotting density, chart size, scale and aspect ratio affect how viewers decode charts.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Supports bar and column charts over pies and bubbles when the audience must compare magnitudes.
- **Limitations:** PDF pages 1-3 read. Exp. 1A uses simple two-mark percent-difference judgments with crowd workers, so the ranking is established for that task. Results for Exp. 1B (rectangular area), Exp. 2 (gridline contrast) and Exp. 3 (chart size and gridline spacing) were not read. The 1984 Cleveland & McGill original was not accessed.
- **Implication for the skill:** For comparing values, position on a common baseline (bars or dots on one shared axis) is the most accurate encoding; length is next; angle (pie) and area (bubble) are less accurate. Default to bars for value comparison and fix chart size and aspect ratio rather than assuming they do not matter.

### B2. Truncating the Y-Axis: Threat or Menace?

- **Source:** Michael Correll (Tableau Research), Enrico Bertini (New York University), Steven Franconeri (Northwestern University); CHI 2020, 2020-01-08 (arXiv v2; CHI 2020 version), <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.02035>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Correll, Bertini & Franconeri: bar charts whose y-axis does not start at zero can visually exaggerate effect sizes. Truncating a bar chart's axis breaks the convention that bar-height differences are proportional to value differences. In crowd-sourced experiments the subjective impact of axis truncation persisted across visualization designs, even when designs explicitly cued that truncation had occurred. The authors recommend that designers consider the scale of the meaningful effect sizes and variation they intend to communicate, regardless of the visual encoding. They also note that when trends rather than individual values are the message, not truncating the y-axis can in some cases itself be perceived as deceptive.
- **Limitations:** arXiv v2 preprint, pages 1-2 read. Sample sizes and effect-size statistics were not read; the CHI 2020 camera-ready may differ. Measures are subjective perceived effect size, not decision accuracy. Confidence is high for bar charts only; the evidence for other chart types is less direct. The Tableau CHI publication page returned HTTP 403.
- **Implication for the skill:** Hard rule: bar and column charts start at zero. Line charts are different (see B3). For any non-zero scale, show the axis range and state the magnitude of differences in numbers.

### B3. Why our column and bar charts start at zero (or below)

- **Source:** Datawrapper (vendor academy page), 2021-03-23 (last updated, per page), <https://www.datawrapper.de/academy/why-our-column-and-bar-charts-start-at-zero>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Datawrapper's guidance: column and bar charts whose y-axis starts above zero are considered misleading, and its tool does not allow it. Dot and range plots may start at a non-zero value because they have no filled bar. Line charts can but do not need to start at zero. For ordered categories such as years or months, plotting differences between values is an option. Datawrapper also reports that the 2015 CHI study by Pandey et al. found readers of a deceptive column chart perceived the underlying message in its exaggerated form (a secondary report, not verified against the paper). Correll et al. (B2) quote Alberto Cairo that a zero baseline is usually advisable when the encoding is height or length, while line charts (position and angle) are not distorted by a baseline closer to the first data point, and quote Bergstrom & West that line graphs need not include zero.
- **Limitations:** Vendor guidance, not a controlled study. Confidence is high for the bar and column zero baseline; the line-chart exceptions are medium. The Pandey et al. 2015 finding is only a secondary report; the ACM page returned HTTP 403. The Cairo and Bergstrom & West statements were read in the Correll et al. preprint (B2).
- **Implication for the skill:** Column and bar charts: zero baseline (hard rule). Line charts: non-zero allowed when the change is the message; label the axis range. Dot and range plots: non-zero allowed.

### B4. Datawrapper blog post on dual-axis charts

- **Source:** Datawrapper (vendor blog), 2018-05-08 (page also carries a 2026 edit), <https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/dualaxis>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** practitioner-guidance | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Datawrapper argues that dual-axis charts should be avoided because their scales are arbitrary and can deliberately mislead, and because items placed close together look related even when far apart in value. It cites Isenberg et al. (2011, described as a 15-participant study) finding that the superimposed dual-axis design performed poorly on both accuracy and time. Recommended alternatives: side-by-side charts with one axis per series; indexed charts showing change from a base value, with labels or tooltips for absolute values (useful when series change at similar rates); and prioritizing the main series while annotating the other. The author says the first two alternatives are useful in most cases.
- **Limitations:** The empirical basis is a single 2011 study cited only through Datawrapper; it was not read and its venue is unverified (the search suggested it is not a CHI paper). A search snippet (Lo et al. 2022, not accessed) reports axis choice as the most common misleading feature in a 1,143-chart corpus (about 28.6%); this is unverified. No controlled CHI dual-axis experiment was found.
- **Implication for the skill:** Executive materials: do not use dual y-axes. Use two small charts, or index both series to a common base period and label the endpoints.

### B5. Arcs, Angles, or Areas: Individual Data Encodings in Pie and Donut Charts

- **Source:** Drew Skau and Robert Kosara (UNC Charlotte; Tableau Research); EuroVis 2016, Computer Graphics Forum 35(3):121-130, DOI 10.1111/cgf.12888, 2016, <https://media.eagereyes.org/papers/2016/Skau-EuroVis-2016.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Skau & Kosara Study 1 (Mechanical Turk; 102 recruited, 92 analysed) compared six two-segment charts showing a share of 67% (and a 33% mirror): baseline pie, baseline donut, arc length only, angle only (pie and donut), and area only. Mean log error (lower is better; Table 1): baseline pie 1.032 (CI +/-0.138), baseline donut 1.000 (+/-0.137), arc length 1.294 (+/-0.128), area 1.306 (+/-0.125), angle-only pie 1.967 (+/-0.167), angle-only donut 2.279 (+/-0.157); ANOVA F(5,4650)=121.955, p<0.001. The abstract states that angle is the least important visual cue for both chart types and that the donut is as accurate as the traditional pie. The paper also says exploded pies keep the wedge encodings but make the arcs discontinuous; varying-radius and icon-based pies often break accurate arc length or area; nested donuts are harder to compare across layers; and non-circular shapes add error. The authors cite Eells (1926) self-reports (51% arc length, 25% area, 23% angle, 1% chord length) and Cleveland (1994), who argues pie charts are inferior for many common tasks because of degraded pattern perception.
- **Limitations:** Two-segment stimuli at one share (67%, with a 33% mirror), crowd sample, and 16 participants whose angle answers were corrected for opposite-segment answers (the paper reports both corrected and uncorrected results). Study 2 (donut hole size) was read only in the abstract. Confidence is high for the ordering and medium for exact values, which are from Table 1 as printed.
- **Implication for the skill:** Pies: use only for two or three labeled part-to-whole slices. Do not use exploded, varying-radius, icon-shaped or nested pies. Prefer sorted bars for shares. A donut is not penalised relative to a pie on this task.

### B6. Useful Junk? The Effects of Visual Embellishment on Comprehension and Memorability of Charts

- **Source:** Scott Bateman, Regan L. Mandryk, Carl Gutwin, Aaron Genest, David McDine, Christopher Brooks (University of Saskatchewan); CHI 2010, pp. 2573-2582, 2010-04, <https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Bateman et al. compared Holmes-style embellished charts with plain versions of the same charts, measuring interpretation accuracy and recall. Interpretation accuracy did not differ. Recall after a gap of roughly two to three weeks (longer than 12 days) was significantly better for the embellished charts. Eye tracking showed participants spent time on the non-data ink in embellished charts, and participants overwhelmingly preferred the embellished style. The authors frame Tufte's data-ink ratio (remove ink not used to present data) as the minimalist position they test. They say they are cautious about recommending that all charts be produced this way, and note that images can intentionally or unintentionally bias the viewer's reading of the data.
- **Limitations:** Pages 1-2 read only. Participant count (a secondary Medium summary says 20), test statistics and effect sizes were not read. Small set of charts from one designer.
- **Implication for the skill:** Restrained, data-relevant imagery did not cost accuracy in this test and can aid recall. For analytical executive material, keep embellishment minimal and never let imagery replace labels or axes.

### B7. What Makes a Visualization Memorable?

- **Source:** Michelle A. Borkin, Azalea A. Vo, Zoya Bylinskii, Phillip Isola, Shashank Sunkavalli, Aude Oliva, Hanspeter Pfister; IEEE TVCG (InfoVis 2013), 2013, <https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20130101-what-makes-a-visualization-memorable>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Borkin et al. scored 2,070 single-panel visualizations (news sites, government reports, scientific journals, infographics) for memorability via Mechanical Turk. Observers were consistent about which graphics were memorable and which forgettable. Attributes such as color and a recognizable object enhance memorability. Common graphs are less memorable than unique visualization types. Each graphic was also annotated for data-ink ratio and visual density; the page reports no finding about how those affect memorability.
- **Interpretation (agent):** The memorability finding favors novelty, which conflicts with decodability; in executive materials decodability should win except for the single highlighted message.
- **Limitations:** Read only the publication-page summary; the PDF URL returned HTTP 404 in this session. No effect sizes or sample statistics read. Memorability was measured for single images, not decision accuracy.
- **Implication for the skill:** Memorability and comprehension are different objectives. For analysis, use standard, quickly decodable chart types; reserve color or a recognizable object for the one message the audience must remember.

### B8. Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics

- **Source:** Ha-Kyung Kong (UIUC), Zhicheng Liu (Adobe Research), Karrie Karahalios (UIUC); CHI 2018, DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174012, 2018-04, <https://mail.zcliu.cs.umd.edu/vistitles/CHI18-VisTitles.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Kong, Liu & Karahalios: participants wrote visualization titles using five news frames (conflict, economic, human interest, morality, responsibility), an open-ended frame and a statistics frame. In a second study, title slant strongly shaped the perceived main message of the same visualization, so viewers derived opposing messages from one chart. Slant and attitude-consistency did not significantly change attitudes. Most viewers believed visualizations are neutral, and the authors warn that subtle statistics framing can go unnoticed.
- **Interpretation (agent):** Test: if the title would read differently under the opposing frame, rewrite it.
- **Limitations:** Pages 1-2 of the preprint read. Sample sizes and Study 2 details not read. Topics were politically controversial (Syrian refugees, military budget), so the slant effects are shown for those topics.
- **Implication for the skill:** Titles are read as the message. Write the title as a factual takeaway without evaluative adjectives or framing; check that someone who disagrees with the conclusion would read the same claim from the title and chart.

### B9. Towards Understanding How Readers Integrate Charts and Captions: A Case Study with Line Charts

- **Source:** Dae Hyun Kim, Vidya Setlur, Maneesh Agrawala; CHI 2021, 2021-01-20 (arXiv submission), <https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08235>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Kim, Setlur & Agrawala (crowdsourced, line charts): readers adopt a caption's highlighted feature as the takeaway when the chart also visually emphasizes that feature. When the caption points to a low-prominence feature, readers rely on the chart and report its more prominent feature instead. Adding external context to a caption helped readers take away the caption's message.
- **Limitations:** Read the arXiv abstract page only. Number of charts and participants not read. Line charts only. A search snippet misattributed the paper to a different author list.
- **Implication for the skill:** Make the chart's most visually prominent feature match the intended takeaway (highlight color, annotation). A caption cannot override a more dominant pattern; if the chart's dominant pattern differs from the message, fix the chart.

### B10. Declutter and Focus: Empirically Evaluating Design Guidelines for Effective Data Communication

- **Source:** Kiran Ajani, Elsie Lee, Cindy Xiong, Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, William Kemper, Steven Franconeri; IEEE TVCG 2021, 2021, <https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10350268>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper & Franconeri tested six graph topics in three versions: cluttered; decluttered (non-critical gridlines, excess data labels and color variability removed); and decluttered plus focused (a headline describing the relevant pattern, a subset of values in a unique color, and annotations). Decluttered designs received higher professionalism ratings. Adding focus raised aesthetics and clarity ratings and gave better memory for the highlighted pattern across redraws and typed conclusions. The authors state they do not know whether these results generalize beyond memory-based tasks. Kellogg Insight's summary adds that decluttering alone did not significantly improve redrawing or written conclusions, and that decluttered-plus-focused graphs were about 2.5 times as likely as decluttered-only graphs to convey the main takeaway in writing.
- **Limitations:** Read the paper's abstract and Figure 1 page only. The 2.5x figure and sample details come from the Kellogg Insight summary (24 participants, six graphs, 10-second viewing), which is secondary (https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/do-people-understand-your-data-visualizations). The sample is described differently across sources (Northwestern-affiliated adults in Kellogg; undergraduates in a search snippet). Small sample.
- **Implication for the skill:** Decluttering alone improves perceived professionalism, not necessarily comprehension. Default sequence: remove gridlines and redundant labels, use one highlight color, add a headline that states the pattern and an annotation at the key point. Use no more than two colors, ideally one (Franconeri's advice as reported by Kellogg).

### B11. The Science of Visual Data Communication: What Works

- **Source:** Steven L. Franconeri, Lace M. Padilla, Priti Shah, Jeffrey M. Zacks, Jessica Hullman; Psychological Science in the Public Interest 22(3):110-161, DOI 10.1177/15291006211051956, 2021-12, <https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/15291006211051956/>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** meta-analysis-or-review | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks & Hullman, reviewing evidence on visual data communication. Per the abstract: extracting global statistics from a graphic is fast, but comparing between subsets of values is slow; effective graphics avoid taxing working memory, guide attention and follow familiar conventions; flawed charts can produce confusion and distrust, especially among readers with limited graph literacy; and visuals should be customized to the audience.
- **Limitations:** Only the journal's abstract page was read. Confidence is high for the abstract claims only; the full text is paywalled (SAGE returned HTTP 403), and the review's chart-type-specific recommendations were not read.
- **Implication for the skill:** Design for working memory: one comparison per chart, the summary value visible immediately (title or direct label), conventional chart forms. Customize for the audience.

### B12. Error Bars Considered Harmful: Exploring Alternate Encodings for Mean and Error

- **Source:** Michael Correll, Michael Gleicher (University of Wisconsin-Madison); IEEE TVCG 20(12):2142-2151, DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346298, 2014-08-11 (published online); TVCG Dec 2014, <https://vis.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Corell-ErrorBarsConsideredHarmful.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** Correll & Gleicher reviewed 46 InfoVis papers (2010-2013) that showed sample means with uncertainty. 36 of them (about 78%) used error bars for some notion of spread, and 26 (about 56%) used bar charts with error bars; in 22 papers (about 48%) the error bars were unlabeled. They argue that bar charts with error bars invite a visual containment bias (values inside the bar look more likely than values outside it) and that error bars are ambiguous about what they encode. Crowd-sourced experiments show that the encoding of mean and error significantly changes viewers' decisions about uncertain data. The authors recommend gradient plots (transparency encodes uncertainty) and violin plots (width encodes density) as better alternatives for inferential tasks than bar charts with error bars.
- **Limitations:** Pages 1-2 read (introduction and background). Confidence is high for the review counts; the experimental claims are medium confidence because experimental designs, sample sizes and effect sizes were not read. Gradient and violin plots are uncommon in executive decks, so the recommendation needs adaptation.
- **Implication for the skill:** Label what every uncertainty encoding represents (CI, SD, range). For decision-relevant uncertainty, prefer labeled intervals or distributions over bars with whiskers, and state the plain-language meaning. Avoid designs that let a bar read as a container of likely values.

### B13. Understanding SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast; Understanding SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (W3C WCAG 2.1)

- **Source:** W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, non-text-contrast page shows 'Updated 06 September 2026'; use-of-color page shows 'Updated 16 September 2025', <https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html ; https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** standard | **Confidence:** high
- **Finding:** W3C WCAG 2.1 Understanding documents. SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (Level AA): the visual presentation of parts of graphics required to understand the content must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s); the document calls the 3:1 threshold strict (2.999:1 fails). Exceptions include essential presentations, cases where embedded or overlaid text conveys the same information (that text must then meet text contrast), decorative graphics, and content also provided in another form such as a data table. SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A): color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element. Its examples are required form fields marked with red text plus an icon, and a chemical diagram identifying elements by both color and numbers with a legend.
- **Limitations:** WCAG is written for web content; applying it to slides and printed charts is an extension. The Understanding documents are informative, not normative.
- **Implication for the skill:** Hard rules: (1) every bar, line or segment a reader needs must reach 3:1 against its adjacent background or neighbouring color; (2) never encode a category, sign or variance by color alone; add a label, direct label, sign, arrow or pattern.

### B14. Fundamentals of Data Visualization, Chapter 19: Common pitfalls of color use

- **Source:** Claus O. Wilke (book chapter); Okabe & Ito, Color Universal Design page (https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/), unknown (no date on page), <https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/color-pitfalls.html>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** book | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Wilke (Fundamentals of Data Visualization, ch. 19) states that approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females have some color-vision deficiency. He recommends the Okabe-Ito eight-color qualitative palette: orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73, yellow #F0E442, blue #0072B2, vermilion #D55E00, reddish purple #CC79A7, black #000000. He notes that red and green give the strongest contrast for normal vision but are nearly indistinguishable for red-green deficiency, and recommends direct labeling instead of colors when distinguishing more than about eight categorical items; colors are also harder to tell apart on thin lines and small elements. Okabe & Ito's own page (jfly.uni-koeln.de/color) describes the palette as unambiguous for both colorblind and non-colorblind readers, avoids colors between yellow and green, and advises darker blue and orange rather than sky blue and yellow for thin lines and small characters.
- **Limitations:** The prevalence figure is stated on the page without a citation. The Okabe & Ito page shows the palette only as an image, so the hex values come from Wilke's page. A search snippet (not accessed) reports about 8% of men and about 0.4% of women of European Caucasian ancestry for Birch (2012); see disagreements.
- **Implication for the skill:** Categorical palette: at most eight colors, Okabe-Ito hex values, direct labels beyond about eight categories, never red and green as the only distinction.

### B15. Communication Breakdown (IBCS article, AFP Exchange); ISO 24896 pages on ibcs.com

- **Source:** Andrej Lapajne (AFP Exchange); IBCS Association (ibcs.com; ISO page https://www.ibcs.com/iso-24896/), 2015-11 (article); 2026-06-11 (ISO 24896 publication, per ibcs.com), <https://www.ibcs.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/AFP_Exchange_Lapajne_Seiten_43-47_aus_November_2015.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** firm-publication | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Lapajne (AFP Exchange, November 2015) describes the International Business Communication Standards (IBCS) as consistently defining the shapes and colors of actuals and budgets, variances and different KPIs, and gives an IBCS-compliant P&L example with absolute and relative variance columns (delta PY and delta PY%). The IBCS site says ISO 24896 'establishes a standardized visual notation for business reporting' and reports its publication on June 11, 2026; IBCS Standards Version 2.0 is described as aligned with it.
- **Limitations:** The SUCCESS rule letters, the zero-baseline rule and the scenario encodings were not verified against primary IBCS or ISO text. Search snippets (not accessed) describe scenario encodings (actual solid black, prior year grey, plan outlined, forecast hatched) and green/red for favourable/unfavourable variance; treat as unverified. The IBCS rules video page had no transcript.
- **Implication for the skill:** Within one document, keep one fixed encoding per scenario (actual, plan, prior year, forecast) and show variances as both absolute and relative with sign and favourability. Verify the specifics against the primary IBCS 2.0 or ISO 24896 text before writing them into the skill as rules.

### B16. Graphical Perception of Multiple Time Series; Face to Face: Evaluating Visual Comparison

- **Source:** Waqas Javed, Bryan McDonnel, Niklas Elmqvist (Purdue; IEEE TVCG 2010); Brian Ondov, Nicole Jardine, Niklas Elmqvist, Steven Franconeri (InfoVis 2018), 2010-10 (Javed, posted online); 2018 (Ondov), <https://cs.au.dk/~elm/pdf/multilinevis.pdf ; https://cs.au.dk/~elm/pdf/face2face.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** yes | **Evidence type:** empirical-study | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Javed, McDonnel & Elmqvist (IEEE TVCG 2010): separate-panel techniques (small multiples, horizon graphs) are generally more efficient for comparisons across time series over a large visual span, while shared-space techniques (standard line graphs) are typically more efficient for comparisons over smaller visual spans where overlap and clutter are reduced. Ondov, Jardine, Elmqvist & Franconeri (InfoVis 2018, crowdsourced): overlaid layouts performed strongly compared with standard juxtaposed small multiples on maximum-delta and correlation comparison tasks, confirming prior intuition; mirror-symmetric small multiples and animated transitions improved performance further.
- **Limitations:** Abstract and first page of each PDF read; detailed accuracy figures were not read. The two studies used different tasks and measures, so the results apply to the tasks tested.
- **Implication for the skill:** Two-series comparisons with close values: overlay in one panel (Ondov). Many series or a wide visual span: separate small multiples with a shared axis range (Javed).

### B17. Information Visualization: Marks & Channels (lecture slides)

- **Source:** Tamara Munzner (University of British Columbia); see also Visualization Analysis and Design (2014), lecture slides, 2016-2020 versions; date unknown for this copy, <https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/533/slides/chap3.pdf>
- **Accessed this session:** no | **Evidence type:** expert-opinion | **Confidence:** medium
- **Finding:** Munzner's lecture slides (UBC) state the expressiveness principle (encode all, and only, the information in the data attributes) and the effectiveness principle (the salience of a channel should track the importance of the attribute it carries). They list magnitude channels from position on a common scale, through unaligned position, length, angle and area, and identity channels such as spatial region, hue, motion and shape, with accuracy, discriminability, separability and popout as the ranking criteria. The slides attribute the accuracy ranking to Cleveland and McGill and its crowdsourced replication to Heer and Bostock.
- **Limitations:** Not accessed: the chapter PDF exceeds the fetch size limit, so the wording and ranking come from search-result snippets. A second copy (cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/436V-20/slides/marks.pdf) was read only for its title and one slide. The exact middle order (angle, area, length) was not verified against the book, and secondary sources differ on it.
- **Implication for the skill:** Use the most accurate channel for the single most important quantity; never use a channel that implies order for unordered categories. Verify the exact ranking against the book before writing it into the skill.

## Disagreements

- **Non-zero baseline for line charts versus trend messages:** Datawrapper and Cairo (as quoted by Correll et al.): non-zero baselines are acceptable for line charts. Correll et al.: truncation exaggerates effects in bar charts; for trend-focused messages, not truncating a y-axis can in some cases itself be perceived as deceptive. The rule therefore depends on the message being communicated, not only on chart type. (B2, B3)
- **Pie charts: which perceptual channel readers use, and how pies rank:** Cleveland & McGill-type rankings (as summarized by Munzner slide snippets) place angle below position and length. Skau & Kosara find angle-only pies the least accurate, donut about equal to pie, and report self-reports that most readers use arc length, not angle. So the mechanism for pies is contested; the practical conclusion (label values, avoid angle-only designs) is not. (B1, B5, B17)
- **Overlay versus small multiples:** Javed et al. (2010): separate panels are more efficient for comparisons across a large visual span; shared-space line graphs are more efficient over small spans. Ondov et al. (2018): overlaid layouts performed strongly against standard small multiples on maximum-delta and correlation tasks. Results are task- and span-dependent rather than contradictory. (B16)
- **Strength of evidence against dual-axis charts:** Practitioner guidance (Datawrapper) is strongly against dual axes, citing one 2011 study. Search snippets (not accessed) suggest axis choice is the most common misleading feature in a 1,143-chart corpus. No controlled CHI dual-axis experiment was found. The practitioner consensus is stronger than the empirical base. (B4)
- **Decluttering: what it improves:** Ajani et al. abstract: decluttering improves professionalism ratings; focus improves aesthetics, clarity and memory. Kellogg's summary of the same study: decluttering alone did not significantly improve redrawing or written conclusions. The sample is also described differently (24 Northwestern-affiliated participants in Kellogg; undergraduate students in a search snippet). (B10)
- **Embellishment:** Bateman et al.: embellished charts were no less accurate to read and were better recalled after 2-3 weeks. Minimalist position (Tufte's data-ink principle, as framed by the authors): non-data ink should be removed. The authors themselves caution against recommending embellished charts generally. (B6, B7)
- **Prevalence of color-vision deficiency in women:** Wilke: about 0.5% of females. Birch (2012), as summarized in a search snippet and not accessed: about 0.4% of females of European Caucasian ancestry, about 8% of males in both. Use a range or cite the specific source. (B14)

## Implementation recommendations

- [core] Hard rule: bar and column charts start the value axis at zero; never truncate them in executive materials. (B2, B3). Truncated bars exaggerate differences and break the proportional-length convention; the effect persists even with explicit cues. Practitioner guidance agrees.
- [core] Comparison question (values across categories): use sorted horizontal or vertical bars on one shared, zero-based axis, with direct value labels. (B1, B5). Position on a common scale and length are the most accurate encodings for comparing values; angle and area are less accurate.
- [core] Trend question (change over time): use a line chart. A non-zero baseline is allowed when the change is the message; print the axis range and label the start and end values. If magnitude matters to the decision, use a zero baseline or state the scale in numbers. (B2, B3). Zero is not required for lines, but truncation can exaggerate and non-truncation can mislead when the trend is the message.
- [core] Composition question (part-to-whole): prefer sorted bars or a 100% stacked bar with direct labels. A pie or donut is allowed only for two or three labeled slices. Never use exploded, varying-radius, icon-shaped or nested pies, and never encode a share by angle alone. (B5). Angle-only encodings are least accurate; donuts are as accurate as pies; the paper reports added error for exploded, varying-radius, icon and nested variants.
- [reference] Distribution and relationship questions (histograms, box plots, violins, scatter plots): no chart-type evidence was gathered in this stream. Use the labeled-interval rule for uncertainty and mark any other defaults as unverified in the skill. (B12). Research gap; avoid presenting unverified defaults as evidence-based.
- [optional] Variance-to-target and scenario questions (actual vs plan vs prior year): keep one fixed encoding per scenario across the document; show absolute and relative variance together; mark favourability with sign and text as well as color. Verify the specifics against primary IBCS 2.0 or ISO 24896 text before making them hard rules. (B15). IBCS standardizes shapes and colors of actuals, budgets and variances; the specific scenario codes and rules are not verified here.
- [core] Multi-series comparison: overlay series in one panel when values are close and the span is modest; use separate small multiples with a shared axis range when there are many series or a wide visual span. (B16). Task- and span-dependent results from Javed et al. (2010) and Ondov et al. (2018); no numeric series-count threshold was found in the sources.
- [core] Uncertainty: label what every interval encodes (CI, SD, range). For decision-relevant uncertainty prefer labeled intervals or distributions (gradient or violin) over bars with whiskers; never let bars imply that values inside them are the likely ones. (B12). Unlabeled error bars were common in InfoVis papers and viewers' decisions change with the encoding.
- [core] Titles and captions: state the takeaway as a factual, neutral sentence with no evaluative adjectives; test whether the title would read differently under an opposing frame; ensure the chart's most prominent feature supports the same takeaway. (B8, B9). Slanted titles change the perceived main message; captions are overridden by more prominent chart features.
- [core] Focus after decluttering: remove non-critical gridlines, redundant labels and extra colors, then add one highlight color on the key series, a headline that describes the pattern, and one annotation at the key point. Do not present decluttering alone as improving comprehension. Use no more than two colors, ideally one. (B10, B11). Focus improved memory and clarity; decluttering alone improved professionalism ratings but not conclusions in one study.
- [core] Hard color rule: every chart mark a reader needs (bars, lines, segments, markers) must reach a 3:1 contrast ratio against its adjacent color, including against a white background. Never encode a category, sign or variance by color alone; add a direct label, sign, arrow or pattern. (B13). WCAG 1.4.11 (AA, strict 3:1) and 1.4.1 (Level A).
- [core] Categorical palette: use the Okabe-Ito eight-color set (hex values in B14), and at most about eight categories. On a white background, do not use yellow #F0E442 (about 1.3:1), orange #E69F00 or sky blue #56B4E9 (each about 2.3:1) as the only color of a line or bar. Prefer blue #0072B2 (about 5.2:1), vermilion #D55E00 (about 3.9:1), bluish green #009E73 (about 3.4:1), reddish purple #CC79A7 (about 3.1:1) or black. Beyond about eight categories, use direct labels instead of more colors. (B13, B14). Okabe-Ito is designed for color-vision deficiency; Wilke recommends it and direct labeling beyond about eight categories. The contrast ratios are computed by the research agent from the WCAG relative-luminance formula, not taken from a source; the validator should recompute them.
- [core] Never use red and green as the only distinction between categories or between positive and negative variance. (B14). About 8% of males and roughly 0.4-0.5% of females have color-vision deficiency; red-green pairs become nearly indistinguishable for them.
- [core] Dual y-axes: do not use in executive materials. Use side-by-side charts, or index both series to a common base period and label the values. (B4). Practitioner guidance against dual axes citing one 2011 study; the controlled evidence base is thin.
- [optional] Embellishment and memorability: restrained, data-relevant imagery may be used for recall, but keep it out of the analytical exhibit and never let it replace labels or axes. Use unusual chart forms only where the single message must be remembered; standard chart types remain the default for analysis. (B6, B7). Embellishment did not reduce accuracy and improved recall in one study; memorability favors unusual chart types and images can bias readers.
