## Gap fills (validator research)

- **AV1** Primary source for the 'seven percentage points' claim. Lovallo & Sibony surveyed managers on 1,048 major decisions (new products, M&A, large capex) and rated 17 practices (eight about quantity and detail of analysis, the rest about decision process). In a multivariate regression, process quality explained 53% of variance in decision-making effectiveness, analysis 8% and industry/company variables 39% ('process mattered more than analysis by a factor of six'). On the 673 decisions with ROI data, bottom-to-top quartile on process quality = +6.9 pp ROI; top vs bottom quartile on analysis = +5.3 pp. Data are retrospective manager self-reports, and the authors acknowledge halo-effect risk. Process items included exploring major uncertainties and including perspectives that contradict the senior leader. Use as motivation, not as an effect size. The 'factor of six' (variance share) and the ROI gaps (6.9 vs 5.3 pp) tell different stories on magnitude. Source: The case for behavioral strategy (Lovallo & Sibony, McKinsey Quarterly); university-hosted PDF copy because mckinsey.com returned 503, 2010-03, <https://cdn.docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid405226.pdf>. Why critical: This is the only quantified basis for the process-over-analysis theme that justifies alternatives, dissent and uncertainty rules, and the seven-point figure is the number most likely to be quoted wrongly in deliverables.
- **AV2** Original RAPID article (Rogers & Blenko, HBR Jan 2006, hosted by Bain) presents Agree as a sign-off role that can carry veto power, with limits: 'only a few should have such veto power', and 'if someone exercises a veto, he or she must offer an alternative or escalate the issue to the person with the D'. The D is the 'single point of accountability'. This contradicts Bain's 2026 article (A11), which says A has no veto and disagreement goes to the D. A rule must therefore have the document state each sign-off party's blocking power and escalation path explicitly instead of assuming a definition. Source: Who Has the D? (Rogers & Blenko), Bain & Company page; read through a summarising fetch, 2006-01, <https://bain.com/insights/who-has-the-d>. Why critical: Resolves the dossier's role-definition disagreement toward a safer rule and prevents Claude from asserting a RAPID definition that Bain's own two articles contradict.
- **AV3** Evidence behind the outside-view rule. Flyvbjerg reports that cost forecasts for transport infrastructure were on average 44.7% too low for rail, 33.8% for bridges and tunnels and 20.4% for roads (constant prices), with no improvement over about 70 years; rail passenger forecasts averaged -51.4% error. He describes reference class forecasting (identify a reference class, build the distribution of outcomes, place the project in it), which the American Planning Association endorsed, and claims transport projects are no worse than other project types. Evidence is large-sample but from public infrastructure; transfer to corporate revenue or timeline forecasts is by analogy. Source: From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right (Flyvbjerg, Project Management Journal 37(3)); arXiv copy, 2006, <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3642>. Why critical: The HBR checklist's claim that inside-view forecasts are too optimistic was an assertion in the dossier; this is the data that supports the base-case benchmarking rule.
- **AV4** Evidence on claim-style (sentence) slide headlines. Farkas's critique of Alley's work reports that Alley et al. (2006) found statistically significant but 'surprisingly modest' retention gains for sentence headlines, in a university course, with weak control slides and several changes made at once. Farkas still sees 'a good case for sentence-style slide titles': they focus attention on one idea and audiences remember a statement better as a title than as a bullet. He cautions that long sentence titles constrain a live speaker, and that requiring them suits decks people read or must present from. The primary Alley paper was not read. Source: Assessment of Alley's slide design (David K. Farkas, informal critique, 2010), discussing Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell & Muffo (Technical Communication 53(2), 2006), 2010, <https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/dfpubs/Farkas-Assessment%20Of%20Alleys%20Slide%20Design.pdf>. Why critical: The dossier admitted there was no evidence that claim headings aid executive comprehension. This gives the only empirical anchor, modest and confounded, and scopes the rule to read-ahead decks and reports.

## Exclusions

- **Printing the 12-question HBR checklist or Spetzler's six elements inside deliverables:** They are review aids for a reviewer or for the author's own pre-send check. Showing them adds length without helping the decision maker. Keep them as an internal check only.
- **Quoting the McKinsey seven-point ROI figure, 'process 6x analysis', Klein's 30%, Schoemaker's 50% or Nutt's 'half fail' as headline evidence:** Self-reported correlational survey, second-hand/untraceable figure, single small experiment, and an implementation-based failure definition. Allowed only with definition, source and limitation.
- **Mandatory or visible MECE (labels, rigid exclusivity):** Critiques are conceptual (relevance, over-constraint, useful redundancy), and no study shows rigid MECE improves outcomes. Keep only as an internal check.
- **RAPID letters and the RAPID framework as a required structure:** Bain's 2006 and 2026 articles define Agree differently. The roles are useful, but the document should use plain labels and state blocking power explicitly.
- **Asserting '80/20' as a law or default split:** A heuristic that varies by data set; the source is a low-evidence secondary page.
- **Scenario planning as a default step, including the ten-step process:** One small experiment and case-based support; adds length and false precision for most deliverables. Keep as an optional module.
- **A full 'how this was produced' process narrative:** Risks fabricated dissent or consultation when written by a model. Replace with a 1-3 line Basis note limited to facts the user supplied.
- **Premortem or full alternatives treatment for routine decisions:** The HBR authors exclude routine rubber-stamp decisions; the effort is not justified by the evidence.
- **Eisenhardt-style 'use more information' as a general rule:** Eight firms in one 1980s industry, abstract-level read, and the type of information (real-time operating data) is not stated in the abstract. Support only for parallel alternatives, weakly.
- **Dean & Sharfman (1996) as supporting evidence:** Only an abstract-level read; direction and size of effects unknown.
- **Pyramid-principle jargon (governing thought, SCQ) and ghost-deck or storyline-first as named methods in output:** Documented only in practitioner pages; no evidence on effect. The practice (answer first, claim headings) is retained without the vocabulary.
- **Specific issue-tree depth limits or fixed counts of branches:** No evidence found; likely decorative.

## Unresolved contradictions

- RAPID Agree role: Rogers & Blenko 2006 (Bain-hosted) says Agree can carry a limited veto ('only a few should have such veto power'; a vetoing party must offer an alternative or escalate to the D). Bain 2026 says Agree has no veto and unresolved disagreement goes to the D. Third-party summaries differ again. Not resolvable from sources; the rule requires the document to state blocking power explicitly.
- Magnitude of the process effect in the McKinsey survey: process explains 53% of variance vs 8% for analysis ('factor of six') but the ROI gap between top and bottom quartile is 6.9 pp for process vs 5.3 pp for analysis. The two framings suggest different practical importance. The process measure also includes analysis-adjacent practices, and all data are self-reported.
- Claim headlines: Alley et al. report modest but significant retention gains for sentence headlines. Farkas says the study had weak controls and ignored the speaker's role, and that long sentence titles can constrain live delivery. Resolved by scope (read-ahead decks yes, live talks keep short) but no direct test of executive comprehension exists.
- Hypothesis-first (McKinsey practice, unverified) vs the single-hypothesis trap (HBR Q5): the pairing with an explicit disconfirmation step is a synthesis; no study tests that it prevents confirmation bias.
- Breadth vs focus: Eisenhardt and Nutt favour more alternatives and wider search, while 80/20 and 'stop when more analysis would not change the decision' favour focus. Practitioner resolution is breadth of options with depth only where it matters; no evidence found on where to stop.
- Self-review vs independent review: HBR authors say review must be independent of the proposers; Claude can only self-check. The compromise (self-check plus advising independent review for high stakes) has no tested support.
