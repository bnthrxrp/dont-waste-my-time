## Gap fills (validator research)

- **CV1** Adams, Converse, Hales and Klotz (Nature 592:258-261, 8 April 2021) report eight experiments plus observational studies: people default to adding and overlook subtraction. In essay-editing tasks only 17% (S6) and 32% (S7) of add/subtract responses were subtractive; itineraries 28%. An explicit cue to 'add or subtract' raised the share generating a subtractive idea (21% to 48% for improve prompts, 28% to 50% for make-it-worse, 28% to 43% in experiments 2-3, 41% to 61% in experiment 1). Higher cognitive load reduced subtraction (Hedges g=0.18). This is the primary source behind the 83% figure in C3, and it supports an explicit cut prompt as the remedy. Source: People systematically overlook subtractive changes (read via PDF copy at gwern.net), 2021-04-08, <https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/design/2021-adams.pdf>. Why critical: Replaces a book-excerpt statistic with the peer-reviewed primary source and shows what works: a cue to subtract. Limits: mostly online samples and non-business tasks; the essay studies are in the Supplementary Information, which I did not read.
- **CV2** Morkes and Nielsen's Study 3 had 51 experienced web users test five versions of one site with identical information: promotional control, scannable, concise, objective, combined. Measured usability improved 47% (scannable), 58% (concise), 27% (objective) and 124% (combined) over the control. The objective-language gain was significant only for subjective satisfaction; time, errors and memory gains pointed the same way but were not significant. Source: Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web (Nielsen Norman Group), 1997 (page date 1997-01-01; studies run summer 1997), <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/>. Why critical: The dossier had no direct test of concise, scannable or non-promotional writing. This is the only controlled comparison I read that isolates those attributes. Limits: web task, tourism site, 1997, n=51, composite usability metric.
- **CV3** The CAIB primary text (Vol. 1, Aug 2003, p.191 sidebar 'Engineering by Viewgraphs'; p.201) says condensing analyses into slides loses information and that the priority of information 'can be easily misrepresented by its placement on a chart and the language that is used'. The Boeing slide had six levels of hierarchy prioritizing information already in 11 simple sentences. The key fact (debris estimated 640 times larger than the calibration data) appeared only at the bottom. 'Significant(ly)' was used 5 times with meanings from trivial to fatal. Units were written inconsistently, and passive voice, fragments and an ambiguous 'it' weakened the text. At p.201, headings, arrangement and bullet size 'served to highlight what management already believed' and uncertainties dropped out when the analysis was condensed to a verbal brief. Source: Columbia Accident Investigation Board Report Vol. 1, Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 (UNT web archive), 2003-08, <https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/caib/news/report/pdf/vol1/chapters/chapter7.pdf>. Why critical: Primary evidence behind the rules to put the decisive number first, avoid vague quantifiers, and keep risk in full sentences. The slide analysis is Tufte's, quoted by the Board; the Board's own causal account is organizational, so this is a case, not a controlled test. Chapter 8 PDF: https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/caib/news/report/pdf/vol1/chapters/chapter8.pdf
- **CV4** Britt and Larson (2003, J Memory and Language 48:794-810, doi 10.1016/s0749-596x(03)00002-0) report three experiments in which two-clause arguments were read faster when the claim preceded the reason, and claim-first arguments were recalled more accurately than reason-first ones. This is the closest direct experiment on the answer-first ordering that the dossier said did not exist. Source: Constructing representations of arguments (Britt & Larson); bibliographic record confirmed at Crossref, findings read from abstract-level search text, 2003-05, <https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Constructing+representations+of+arguments+Britt+Larson+2003&rows=2>. Why critical: Gives some experimental support for claim-first. Limits: I did not read the paper, only the Crossref record and an abstract-level search result. Short arguments, likely students, no decision-quality outcome. It supports reading speed and recall of the claim, not better decisions.

## Exclusions

- **Hard on-slide text budgets (15 or 20 words per minute, 28 pt headlines, 18-24 pt body):** Values conflict across Penn State sources seen only in search summaries, and the Penn State research page I read contains no such guidance. Not from a study. At most a soft review flag.
- **Westinghouse 'How Managers Read Reports' percentages, '90% chance page ten is unseen', '8x faster innovation cycles':** Second-hand, undated, no sample or primary source found. Folklore.
- **Quoting effect sizes (49 vs 127 words, 4.8% vs 2.7%; g=-0.33; g=-0.70; d=.38; r=.17; 124% usability) in deliverables:** Different contexts, no calibration to executive readers, and several come from secondary summaries. Use as direction only.
- **A rule against highlighted summary boxes:** The only evidence is a 35-student pilot of a methods-section box; the authors said it helped judge the methods quickly. It does not test executive summaries. Answer-first prose is covered by rule 1.
- **Tufte-style absolutism ('PowerPoint is inherently defective', ban slides, ban bullets):** Opinion disputed by commenters and by Farkas. The CAIB supports only the narrower rules on burying risk, nested hierarchy and vague quantifiers.
- **Mandatory visual evidence on every slide and mandatory assertion-evidence template:** Farkas notes experts do not require this; the supporting studies are developer-run with weak controls. Keep sentence titles as a default and add visuals where evidence is quantitative.
- **Claiming memos or BLUF improve decision quality:** No outcome data for Amazon memos; no controlled document-level study for BLUF.
- **The Amazon 2004 Bezos email wording, the 'banned PowerPoint' anecdote, the four-page vs 20-page comparison:** Primary text not read; only the 2017 letter's six-page memo practice is verified.
- **A fixed 'rule of three' or 2-3 supporting arguments:** Not supported by anything read; Minto's constraint is logical grouping (MECE) and order.
- **Full Mayer/Noetel principle list (segmenting, pre-training, modality and others):** Per-principle effects not accessed and learning-lab context. Keep only signaling-like structure, coherence and redundancy ideas already captured.
- **ISO 24495-1 as a compliance or certification claim:** Standard text not read. Use the four principles only as a rubric.
- **Oppenheimer-style 'simple words raise perceived intelligence' as a cited rule:** I read only a press release; it measures perceived author intelligence, not comprehension. The plain-words advice is already covered by the plain-language sentence pass.

## Unresolved contradictions

- Sentence headlines: Alley/Garner studies (developer-run, with deliberately weak controls) vs Farkas (good case for sentence titles, but claims overstated and the speaker's role ignored). Adopted only as a default, with scope by deck mode; the magnitude is unresolved. Alley 2006's confounds are also unresolved (dossier says typography and visuals changed; a search summary says headline was the principal change).
- Cut versus add structure: Rogers and Lasky-Fink advise cutting and also adding headings and a navigation paragraph. Handled by rule (each added element must earn its place), but no threshold exists and the authors concede none.
- Tool versus thinking (Tufte vs commenters): unresolved. The skill takes only the narrow position that placement, hierarchy and vague wording can hide risk.
- On-screen text: Adesope & Nesbit say text plus narration beats narration alone for low-prior-knowledge learners; Yue et al. say verbatim text hurts and a slightly reworded version is best; Roscoe et al. (search-only) found no effect in high schoolers. How much divergence is optimal is unresolved. The deck-mode rule sidesteps it.
- Summary box: the pilot shows no gain in reading time or helpfulness (P=.09, lower), while its authors conclude it helped judge the methods quickly. Unresolved; excluded from rules.
- Sundararajan & Adesope moderators (static details, end placement, print) conflict with a QIC umbrella summary reporting no moderator differences. Unresolved; moderators are not used.
- Signaling effect size (Noetel 'among largest' vs Richter r=0.17 vs Schneider d=.38) unreconciled; not used in rules.
- Memo length: six pages (2017 letter, verified) vs four pages (2004 email, unverified).
- BLUF-first versus contexts where the reader needs background to accept the answer: no evidence found either way; handled by the SCQA exception.
