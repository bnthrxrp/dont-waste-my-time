# Executive writing

Read this for memos, reports, business reviews, board papers, business cases, research briefs, executive emails and executive summaries. It assumes you already write well and covers the structures, templates and cuts that turn a thorough document into one an executive reads and acts on.

## 1. Structures and when to use each

| Structure | Use when | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Answer-first pyramid** | Default for any memo, report or recommendation | One governing thought (the answer, one sentence) → 2-5 key points, each answering the reader's next "why?" or "how?" → evidence under each point |
| **SCQA opening** (a practitioner extension of Minto's SCQ) | The reader lacks the context to accept the answer: a new topic, a counterintuitive answer, a reader outside the work | Situation (what the reader already accepts) → Complication (what changed) → Question (usually implicit) → Answer, in three sentences or fewer; then continue as a pyramid |
| **BLUF** | Emails, updates, status notes, anything read on a phone | Line 1 is the conclusion or what you need from the reader, with the deadline. Then only the background needed to act |
| **Claim → evidence → implication** | Every analytical paragraph | Sentence 1 states the claim; 1-3 sentences of evidence (number, comparison, source); the last sentence says what it means for the reader |
| **What happened / why / so what / now what** | Business reviews, variance commentary, post-mortems | Four layers in this order; label interpretation and estimates so the reader can tell them from facts |

- **Parent test.** Each point summarizes what the points beneath it jointly prove, because it is derived from them: "SMB churn doubled after the March price change", not "Churn analysis".
- **Sibling test.** Items in one group are the same kind (all reasons, all steps or all options) and in a logical order: time, structure or importance. Then check the group for gaps, overlaps and relevance: a complete, non-overlapping list can still hold items that don't change the answer.
- **SCQA only for readers who lack context.** For informed readers it is throat-clearing: go straight to the answer.
- **Moving the answer up is not enough.** Once the conclusion is first, rewrite the headings beneath it as claims too, or the reader still has to assemble the argument.
- **First-sentence test.** Read only the first sentence of each paragraph; the argument should survive. A paragraph with no implication is background: cut it or fold it into the point it supports.
- **Keep the scaffolding out of the deliverable.** Never print "MECE", "SCQA", "pyramid" or "governing thought" in the output; the reader needs the argument, not its method.

## 2. Executive summary rules

For most readers the summary is the document. Write it last; put it first.

- **Synthesize, in full sentences**: the conclusion, the 2-4 reasons with their key numbers, the implication or risk, and the ask with its deadline. A list of fragments drops the causes and caveats that connect them.
- **Stand alone**: a reader who reads nothing else can decide. Define any term it uses. Never send the reader elsewhere for a finding ("see section 3").
- **Never describe the document.** "This report reviews..., Section 2 covers..." is a table of contents, not a summary.
- **Match the body**: no claim or number the body doesn't support, and no caveat in the body that would change the decision but is missing here.

> **Bad:** This report reviews the 2026 promotional program, analyzes performance by channel and sets out recommendations. Section 1 covers spend, Section 2 return on investment and Section 3 next steps.
>
> **Good:** Seven of our twelve national promotions lost money in 2026, a combined −$2.4M in incremental gross profit. All seven were deep-discount events (30% off or more) that pulled demand forward rather than adding to it. Dropping them and moving half their budget into the five profitable events would add about $1.1M in 2027 (estimate; basis: 2026 event-level returns, Appendix B). We need the Commercial Committee's decision by 30 November, before 2027 retailer plans lock.

## 3. Templates

Skeletons, not forms: drop any line that doesn't apply and never pad one to fill it. Field labels (Purpose, Decision required) stay; section headings in the finished document become takeaways.

```
DECISION MEMO
Subject           Decision by [date]: [the decision, in plain words]
Recommendation    one paragraph: what, cost, timing, and the main reason
Why               2-4 takeaway-headed points, each claim → evidence → implication
Options           table: option | benefit | cost | risk | why not chosen (do nothing + at least 1 genuine alternative)
Risks             the 2-3 that could change the decision, each with mitigation and owner; no decorative list
Decision          who decides (one person or body), the exact wording to approve, the deadline, what happens next

PERFORMANCE / BUSINESS REVIEW
Headline          the change that matters most, vs target and vs prior period
Summary           what happened / why / so what / now what, one or two sentences each
Scorecard         KPI | actual | target | var. | prior | var.; highlight exceptions only; partial totals name what they include
Drivers           one takeaway-headed section per material variance, largest first
Outlook           forecast vs target, and the assumption it rests on
Actions           owner | action | date | expected effect

BOARD PAPER: one-page cover sheet, then body
Title             a takeaway, not a topic
Purpose           For decision | For discussion | For noting
Decision required "The Board is asked to approve / endorse / note ..."
Recommendation    one or two sentences
Key financials    cost, benefit, timing, budget status, each with its basis or [TBD: what, owner]
Key risks         top 2-3, with mitigation and residual risk
Consulted         who was consulted and any unresolved dissent, from the user only; else [TBD: who was consulted, owner]
Sign-offs         each required sign-off, and whether it can block the decision
Body              context the Board lacks → analysis → options → recommendation → implementation; detail in appendices

BUSINESS CASE MEMO
The ask           amount / headcount / commitment, decision date, payback or break-even in one line with its basis
Problem           the cost of doing nothing, quantified or [TBD]
Proposal          what, when, who
Economics         cost | benefit | timing | key assumption; each figure sourced, calculated (basis shown) or [TBD: what, owner]
Alternatives      do nothing + at least 1 genuine option, each with why it was not chosen
Risks             what would make it fail; the result if the key assumption is wrong
Success           metric, target, checkpoint date, and the result that would stop or scale it

RESEARCH BRIEF
Question          as the decision-maker would ask it
Answer            the direct answer, confidence (high / medium / low) and why
Findings          3-5 takeaway statements, each with source and evidence status
For us            interpretation, labeled as such; the conditions that would change the answer
Unknowns          what the team must supply (internal data, capabilities, capital), with owner
Sources           title, publisher, date, URL, only for sources actually opened

EXECUTIVE EMAIL / UPDATE
Subject           [Decision | Action | FYI]: the point, with the deadline
Line 1            the ask or the conclusion; one primary ask, others under "Also needed" (owner, date) or in a separate message
Body              2-4 sentences: the key fact or number, why it matters, what is already done
Close             the next step and its date; no sign-off filler

STATUS REPORT
Status            On track | At risk | Off track, with a one-sentence reason
Needs             decisions or help: what, from whom, by when (right after Status, whenever there are any)
Changed           outcomes since the last report, not activities ("held 6 workshops" → what they decided)
Risks / issues    new or changed only, each with owner and date
Next              date | milestone | confidence
```

When a business case lacks its inputs, deliver the structure with placeholders and let the ask carry them: "Hire 4 field technicians at [TBD: annual loaded cost, owner: HR]; payback [TBD: service revenue per technician, owner: Service Ops]". Never put modeled or "illustrative" figures in the ask, the summary or a headline, even with a drafting note elsewhere.

Never invent process either. Consultation, dissent, sign-offs, pilots and validation appear only as the user stated them; otherwise use a placeholder. For each required sign-off, say whether it can block the decision, because decision-rights frameworks disagree on whether a sign-off is a veto.

## 4. Headings, navigation and formatting

- **Headings are takeaways**: a full-sentence claim the section proves. "Market overview" → "Two new entrants price 15% below us, but only in the entry segment".
- **Keep takeaways factual.** Readers take the title as the message, so state the finding without framing or evaluative words ("A strong quarter for pricing"). A reader who disagrees with your conclusion should still read the same claim in the heading.
- **Heading test**: read only the headings, in order; they should tell the argument. A heading that could move unchanged to another document is a label: rewrite it. Reference sections (Appendix, Sources, Method) keep plain labels.
- **Navigation**, for anything over a page: summary first, then one line on where to find what (which section answers which reader's question, what sits in appendices); number the sections and exhibits the text refers to ("Table 2", not "below"); give each appendix a one-line takeaway; use at most two heading levels in a memo.
- **Enough formatting, no more:**
  - Bold the key phrase (a few words: the decision, a key number or a deadline), at most once per section; never whole sentences, and never stack bold, underline and highlight. Each extra emphasis dilutes the others.
  - Use tables for comparisons across two or more dimensions (options × criteria, KPI × period); order rows by importance; put units in the header.
  - Use bullets only for parallel items (options, risks, actions), at most two levels. Keep a chain of reasoning ("because... so... therefore") in prose; bulleting it deletes the logic.
  - State any cause, condition, comparison or material caveat as a full sentence beside the claim it qualifies. A sub-bullet, a footnote or a place at the bottom makes it look minor.
  - No one-sentence sections, decorative callout boxes or emoji.

## 5. Sentence-level rules

| Rule | Instead of | Write |
|---|---|---|
| Front-load the point | "Following a detailed review of regional pricing data, it was found that we are below market" | "We price 8% below the market in every region (September price audit)." |
| One idea per sentence, short sentences | Ideas chained with "and", "which", "while"; sentences past about 25 words | Split at the joint; the second idea gets its own sentence or goes |
| Active voice, named actor, present tense by default; passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant | "It was decided to delay the launch" | "The ExCo moved the launch to May" |
| Verbs, not hidden verbs (-tion, -ment, -ance; make / take / have / give + noun) | "conduct an assessment of", "make a decision on" | "assess", "decide" |
| Numbers with their comparison; for a change, start before end | "Revenue was $12.4M"; "Margin is now 9%, down from 12%" | "Revenue was $12.4M, 3% below plan and 5% above last year"; "Margin fell from 12% to 9%" |
| One term per thing | "net sales", then "revenue", then "turnover" for the same measure | Pick one, define it once, keep it |
| Hedge once, where the doubt is | "This may potentially suggest a possible decline" | "Demand is probably falling (two of three regions, one quarter of data)" |
| Jargon only if the reader uses it | Analyst acronyms in a board paper | The plain term, or the acronym spelled out on first use |

**Quoting research in a deliverable**: only sources opened this session, each with study, year, sample, the definition behind the number and its main limitation. Qualified form: "Lovallo and Sibony (McKinsey Quarterly, 2010): across 1,048 self-reported decisions, companies in the top quartile on decision process earned ROI 6.9 percentage points higher than those in the bottom quartile (673 decisions with ROI data); correlational." Quote Nutt's "half of decisions fail" only with its definition (the decision was not in sustained, full use two years later). Never quote Klein's 30% premortem figure, Schoemaker's scenario-planning figures or Bain's 95%-confidence correlation.

## 6. Anti-fluff

| English filler | Portuguese filler | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| "In today's rapidly changing environment..." | "No cenário atual, cada vez mais dinâmico..." | Delete; open with the fact |
| "It is important to note that...", "It's worth noting..." | "Vale ressaltar que...", "É importante destacar que..." | Delete the frame; state the point |
| "This report aims to provide an overview of..." | "Este documento tem como objetivo apresentar..." | Delete; state the conclusion |
| "This underscores / highlights the importance of..." | "Isso evidencia a importância de..." | Delete; state the consequence and its size |
| "Significant / strong / robust growth" | "Crescimento expressivo / significativo / robusto" | The number and its comparison; keep "significant" for statistical significance |
| "A holistic / comprehensive approach" | "Uma abordagem holística / abrangente" | Say what the approach does |
| "Leverage synergies", "unlock value" | "Alavancar sinergias", "destravar valor" | What combines, worth how much, by when |
| "Going forward", "in the coming period" | "Daqui para frente", "no próximo período" | The date |
| "We will continue to monitor closely" | "Seguiremos acompanhando de perto" | Owner, trigger and date of the next action |
| "Various initiatives are under way" | "Diversas iniciativas estão em andamento" | Name the 2-3 that matter, or count them |
| "As mentioned above", "In summary", "Overall" | "Conforme mencionado", "Em suma", "De modo geral" | Delete; usually the repetition that follows goes too |
| "Please don't hesitate to contact me" | "Fico à disposição para quaisquer dúvidas" | Delete, or state the reply you need |

- **Generic sentence test.** Could the sentence appear unchanged in another company's report, or in this report next quarter? Then it carries no information: rewrite it with the specific fact (which product, what number, who, when) or cut it. "Customer experience remains a top priority" fails; "Complaints per 1,000 orders fell from 4.1 to 2.9 after the courier switch" passes.
- **Say it once.** Each point appears once in the body; the summary may restate it once, compressed. No closing paragraph that recaps the summary: end on the ask or the next step.
- **Superlatives and promotional adjectives become numbers.** "Our best quarter ever" → "Highest quarterly revenue on record: $48M, against a previous high of $45M". "World-class", "game-changing": cut, or replace with the fact behind them.
- **Vague actions become owner + date + what.** "Improve collaboration with Sales" → "Sales Ops shares the weekly pipeline file with Finance from 4 November". If the owner or date is unknown, propose an owner by role where it is obvious ("Sales Ops (proposed)") or leave it out, and list the gap once for the user; never invent a name, and never scatter `[TBD]` markers through the text.
- `python3 ${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/scripts/fluff_lint.py <file>` flags many of these patterns automatically. It is a heuristic: fix real hits, ignore false positives, and don't take a clean run as proof the text is tight.

## 7. Drafting to length and editing existing text

**New documents.** Fix the word budget before drafting, as a ceiling rather than a target, and allocate it across sections; for a one-page memo, roughly a fifth for the recommendation, half for the reasons, the rest for options, risks and the ask. Don't draft long and trim later: the long draft's structure survives the trim. Test every sentence, idea and request: would cutting it change the reader's decision or action? If not, cut it; move detail kept for audit or compliance to an appendix instead of deleting it.

**Existing text.** People improving a text add to it and overlook removal unless prompted to subtract. So prompt yourself: before rewriting, make a cut list that marks every section and paragraph keep, cut or merge with a one-line reason, and ask explicitly what can be removed. Expect the result to be shorter unless something material was missing. Then work from big cuts to small fixes:

1. **Cut throat-clearing and repetition**: preambles, restated questions, "this section covers", pleasantries, closing recaps, the second statement of any point, and secondary requests that compete with the main ask.
2. **Move the answer to the top.** Find the conclusion (often in the last section or a final bullet) and open with it and the ask. If there is none, write it from the evidence, or flag that the document reaches no conclusion.
3. **Convert headings to takeaways** and reorder sections by importance to the decision. Move detail that doesn't change the decision to an appendix.
4. **Check every claim and number** against its source: traceable, compared with something, status labeled, material caveat beside it. Flag errors and inconsistencies; never silently fix them.
5. **Tighten sentences** with sections 5 and 6, then run `fluff_lint.py`.
6. **Check length against the length budgets in briefing.md.** If over, cut in this order: background the reader already has → method (one line; details to an appendix) → second examples of the same point → secondary findings that don't change the decision → padded option or risk lists. Never cut the answer, the ask, a material caveat, the basis of a number or a placeholder.
7. **Read it once as the reader.** Is every element relevant to their decision? Can they find the answer and each argument fast? Is it understandable on first read? Can they act on it (owner, date)?

Keep the author's facts, numbers and defined terms. Deliver the revised text, then a short change log: what moved, what was removed, what was flagged.

## 8. Before/after examples

**English: business review paragraph**

> **Before:** This section provides an overview of third-quarter commercial performance. The quarter was characterized by a challenging environment. Retail, our other channel, delivered revenue in line with plan. In the hospital channel, following the loss of the state tender in August, volumes came in 9% below plan. As a result, total revenue reached $60.2M against a plan of $61.5M. Going forward, the team will continue to monitor the situation closely and take action as appropriate.
>
> **After:** **The hospital tender loss explains the whole Q3 revenue miss of $1.3M (−2.1%).** Revenue was $60.2M against a $61.5M plan. Retail was on plan; hospital volume was 9% below plan after we lost the state tender in August. Unless that volume is replaced, Q4 will miss by a similar amount (estimate: assumes hospital volume stays at its post-tender level). Next step: a plan to replace the tender volume, owned by Commercial (proposed).

What changed: the conclusion, implicit in sentence 5, became the opening takeaway; $1.3M and −2.1% are calculated from stated figures; the forward look is labeled as an estimate with its basis; "continue to monitor" became an action, with placeholders because the source names no owner or date; the generic "challenging environment" sentence is gone. Similar length, far more information.

**Portuguese: e-mail executivo** (112 words → 73, subject line included)

> **Antes:** Prezados, boa tarde! Espero que estejam todos bem. Gostaria de compartilhar algumas atualizações referentes ao projeto de migração do ERP. Vale ressaltar que a equipe vem realizando um trabalho de grande importância. No momento, estamos realizando a validação dos dados, que segue dentro do prazo, e vamos estar enviando o cronograma atualizado na quarta-feira (12/02). Identificamos um atraso de três semanas na integração com o sistema fiscal, o que pode eventualmente vir a impactar o go-live previsto para 1º de março. Nesse sentido, seria importante uma definição da diretoria, até sexta-feira (14/02), quanto à contratação de dois consultores adicionais (R$ 180 mil) para recuperar o prazo. Fico à disposição para quaisquer dúvidas.
>
> **Depois:** *Assunto: Decisão até sexta (14/02): R$ 180 mil para manter o go-live do ERP em 1º/03*
> Prezados, a integração com o sistema fiscal está três semanas atrasada. Para manter o go-live em 1º/03, precisamos que a diretoria aprove até sexta (14/02) a contratação de dois consultores adicionais, por R$ 180 mil. Sem eles, o go-live fica em risco. A validação de dados segue no prazo, e envio o cronograma atualizado na quarta (12/02).

What changed: the ask and its deadline moved from sentence 7 to the subject line and the first paragraph; the praise sentence, the pleasantries, "vale ressaltar", "nesse sentido" and "fico à disposição" are gone; "vamos estar enviando" became "envio"; "pode eventualmente vir a impactar" (three hedges) became "fica em risco"; "uma definição... quanto à contratação" became "aprove a contratação".

## 9. Portuguese (pt-BR) notes

- **Register**: standard written Portuguese, direct and cordial. Avoid bureaucratic formulas ("venho por meio deste", "no aguardo de um retorno", "segue para apreciação") and chat shortcuts ("vc", "blz"). A one-line greeting is fine; skip the pleasantries.
- **No gerundismo**: "vou estar enviando" → "envio" or "vou enviar"; "estaremos realizando a reunião" → "faremos a reunião".
- **Cut the Portuguese-specific filler**: "vale ressaltar / destacar / salientar", "nesse sentido", "no que tange a", "a nível de" (→ "em" or "de"), and "o mesmo" as a pronoun ("o mesmo foi aprovado" → "o plano foi aprovado").
- **Replace weak verb + noun pairs with the verb**: "realizar a análise" → "analisar", "efetuar o pagamento" → "pagar", "fazer a implementação" → "implementar". Don't map English hidden-verb patterns onto Portuguese mechanically.
- **Anglicisms**: keep the ones the audience already uses (forecast, sell-in, go-live, budget); don't add new ones where Portuguese is natural.
- Use pt-BR number and date formats (decimal comma, R$, dd/mm); the detailed locale rules belong to translation.

## 10. Evidence notes

- **Pyramid, parent and sibling tests**: Barbara Minto, "Concept", Minto Books International, undated, https://barbaraminto.com/concept: thinking "ends in a single pyramid of ideas, at many levels, obeying logical rules, and held together by a single thought"; the site brands "The Minto Pyramid Principle and SCQ Framework". "Barbara Minto: 'MECE: I invented it, so I get to say how to pronounce it'", McKinsey & Company Alumni News, undated, https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-insights/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it: "The point above has to be a summary of those below, because it is derived from them"; grouped ideas must be "logically the same, and in logical order". Primary but promotional; no evidence of effect. Neither uses "SCQA", "governing thought" or "answer first": treat SCQA as a common practitioner extension of Minto's SCQ, and the 2-5 count as this skill's design choice (no primary source gives a number).
- **MECE as a check, not a structure**: in the same Alumni profile Minto credits the underlying idea to Aristotle and claims only the abbreviation and its application to groups of ideas. "MECE principle", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECE_principle, summarizes conceptual critiques: it doesn't test relevance, and some overlap can be useful. No outcome study found.
- **Answer first and BLUF**: no controlled comparison of bottom-line-first and chronological documents was found, so treat answer-first as a well-established default, not a measured benefit. "BLUF (communication)", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication), traces the term to US Army Regulation 25-50 and marks its comprehension claim as uncited; the regulation's wording was not verified.
- **Factual, unslanted takeaways**: Kong, Liu and Karahalios, "Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics", CHI 2018, https://mail.zcliu.cs.umd.edu/vistitles/CHI18-VisTitles.pdf. Experiments with about 100 participants each: a title's slant shaped the main message readers took from the same chart. Charts on controversial topics only; the no-evaluative-words rule and its extension to document headings are inferences.
- **Fewer asks, sparing emphasis, navigation for long documents**: Rogers and Lasky-Fink, interview on *Writing for Busy Readers*, Harvard Gazette, October 2023, https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/10/tips-on-how-to-connect-with-people-who-dont-have-time-to-read. The authors advise cutting words, ideas and requests, adding headings and navigation, and limiting emphasis, which they say draws reading to the emphasized part at the expense of the rest. They cite reader surveys and "dozens of randomized experiments" but give no numbers in the interview.
- **Shorter messages**: Rogers and Lasky-Fink, "When Writing for Busy Readers, Less Is More", Behavioral Scientist (book excerpt, undated), https://behavioralscientist.org/when-writing-for-busy-readers-less-is-more/: with 7,002 school board members, a 49-word survey invitation drew a 4.8% response against 2.7% for a 127-word one. No arm sizes or significance tests; one context; directional only, never a benchmark to quote.
- **An explicit cut pass**: Adams, Converse, Hales and Klotz, "People systematically overlook subtractive changes", Nature 592:258-261, 2021, read from a PDF copy, https://www.gwern.net/doc/design/2021-adams.pdf. Across experiments people defaulted to adding, and an explicit cue to consider subtracting raised the share who proposed a removal. Mostly online samples and non-business tasks.
- **Concise, scannable, non-promotional text**: Morkes and Nielsen, "Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web", Nielsen Norman Group, 1997, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/. With 51 users, concise, scannable and objective versions of one site each beat a promotional control on a composite usability measure. One web site, 1997.
- **Caveats and causes in full sentences, the decisive number up top, "significant"**: Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Vol. 1, Chapters 7-8, August 2003, https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/caib/news/report/pdf/vol1/chapters/chapter7.pdf. Drawing on Tufte's analysis, the Board describes a briefing slide whose hierarchy left the key fact at the bottom and used "significant" for meanings from trivial to fatal, and says its headings and arrangement "served to highlight what management already believed". A single case; the Board's causal account is organizational, and the slide is an illustration.
- **Sentence pass and from-to order**: "Plain Language: Writing for understanding", digital.gov, undated, https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing (active voice, present tense, hidden verbs; passive acceptable when the actor is irrelevant; guidance, no comprehension data). Gao and Corter, "Describing and Comprehending Change in Quantitative Information", CogSci 2020, https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0750/index.html: sentences that led with the ending value took longer to process and gave less accurate answers; abstract only, no sample size.
- **Final reader check**: ISO 24495-1:2023, Plain language, Part 1, https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html. Several secondary sources agree it sets four principles: relevant, findable, understandable, usable. The standard's text was not read; use it as a rubric, never as a compliance claim.
- **Genuine alternatives, no decorative risk lists**: Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony, "Before You Make That Big Decision...", Harvard Business Review, June 2011, read from a university-hosted reprint, https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf. Practitioner guidance with no outcome data for its checklist.
- **Quoting decision research**: the qualified Lovallo and Sibony form was read on a McKinsey-hosted copy of the article; the data are self-reported and correlational. Klein's 30% could not be traced to the study it cites, Schoemaker's experiment could not be confirmed from the article page, and Bain gives no method for its 95%-confidence correlation. Bain's own articles also define a sign-off's blocking power differently over time, hence the rule to state it.
- **Prose for reasoning chains**: Amazon, "2017 Letter to Shareholders", https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders, describes six-page narrative memos read silently at the start of meetings. Documented practice, not evidence that memos produce better decisions.
- The sentence-length threshold, the budget allocation and the filler list are this skill's conventions; no accessed source backs them.
